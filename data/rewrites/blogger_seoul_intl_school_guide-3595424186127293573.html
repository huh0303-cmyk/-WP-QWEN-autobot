The usual route from a Korean language course to a Korean degree has three checkpoints: a TOPIK result that meets the university's requirement, an admission letter, and the right student visa. According to Korea's government services portal, TOPIK level 3 or higher is generally required for admission and level 4 or higher for graduation. The rest of this article shows how those pieces fit together and what to confirm at each step.

Two student visas, two different purposes

Study in Korea, the portal run by the National Institute for International Education (NIIED), separates student visas into two main types. The D-2 visa is for people enrolling in degree programmes, from associate degrees to doctorates, or in research. The D-4 visa is for non-degree training, and its sub-types include Korean language training (D-4-1) and foreign language training (D-4-2).

This difference shapes your pathway. If you plan to study Korean first at a language institute and then enter a degree programme, the first stage falls under the non-degree category and the second under the degree category. Each stage has its own admission letter and its own visa conditions, so do not assume that one visa covers both.

The portal also says a D-2 visa is issued for up to two years at a time, with the maximum total stay depending on the programme. For example, it lists up to six years after admission for a bachelor's degree and up to five years for a master's degree. Your university's international office will tell you how extensions are handled in practice.

Where TOPIK fits

NIIED describes TOPIK as a test for non-native speakers and lists university admission, employment and visa qualification among its uses. TOPIK I covers levels 1 and 2, and TOPIK II covers levels 3 to 6. Study in Korea adds that these language requirements vary by type of university and by programme, and that exchange students, scholarship recipients and students in arts and physical education may be treated differently.

The practical rule is simple. Read the language requirement on each programme's admission page, not only the general figure. A university may ask for a higher level for certain majors, accept an English-taught route, or run its own language programme. The sources used here state the general level 3 and level 4 figures but do not list individual universities, so those details must come from the schools themselves.

On timing, Study in Korea lists paper-based tests in January, April, May, July, October and November, internet-based tests in February, March, June, September, October and November, and speaking tests in March, June and October. Check the official TOPIK pages for the exact dates and registration windows of the session you want.

A realistic timeline

The government portal states that applications for the spring semester take place from September to November and for the fall semester from May to June. Counting backwards from a deadline is the safest way to plan:

  1. Find out the application window of each university you are considering.
  2. Choose a TOPIK session whose results will be available before that deadline.
  3. Collect academic records, proof of Korean or English ability and financial documents, which the portal lists as typical application documents.
  4. Submit each application and wait for the admission decision.
  5. Apply for the visa only after you have the admission letter.

Where you take online Korean classes in the meantime matters less than whether your test result arrives on time. If your score falls short, a language course gives you a way to continue studying while you retake the exam, but confirm with the university whether a language-course route to admission exists.

Documents and costs to expect

For a D-2 visa, Study in Korea lists these documents: a passport copy, one passport-size photo taken within the last six months, a copy of the educational institution's business registration certificate, a standard admission letter, a tuberculosis test result where applicable, proof of highest education, and proof of financial ability. The D-4 list is similar but adds a training plan. The portal warns that additional documents may be required depending on the programme and your country, and recommends checking with the Korean diplomatic mission in your country.

The portal also gives approximate visa fees: about 40 US dollars for a single-entry visa for stays of 90 days or less, and about 60 US dollars for a single-entry visa for stays of 91 days or more. Treat these as indicative and confirm the fee with the embassy or consulate that handles your application.

For tuition, the same portal reports average annual undergraduate tuition of about 6.8 million won across national figures and about 7.6 million won at private universities, with engineering and medicine above the humanities average. These are averages, not quotes. Exact fees are on each university's website or on the Academy Info platform that the portal points to.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Using one level for every school. Level 3 is the general figure, not a promise that every programme accepts it.
  • Applying for a visa too early. The portal states that you obtain the student visa after receiving the admission letter.
  • Mixing up D-2 and D-4. A language-training stage and a degree stage are separate categories.
  • Forgetting alien registration. The portal says foreigners staying more than 90 days must register within 90 days of entry.
  • Relying on old test dates. Dates and formats change, so confirm them on the official site before you book.

Rules change: confirm with the official bodies

This is general information, not legal or immigration advice. Visa rules, document lists, fees and language requirements are set by the Ministry of Justice, the Korean embassies and consulates, NIIED and each university, and they can change. Confirm every requirement with the university's admissions office and with the Korean diplomatic mission or immigration office that will handle your case.
