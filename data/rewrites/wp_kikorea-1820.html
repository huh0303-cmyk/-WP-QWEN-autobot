Before viewing any commercial space in Korea, write a one-page description of what the business will do there, because several later steps depend on it: the tax office asks for a lease contract when you register the business, some activities need a permit tied to the premises, and commercial tenancy law treats tenants differently depending on the lease. According to Invest KOREA, the business registration step requires the lease contract, so the address has to be settled before registration can finish.

Why the lease comes early in the setup sequence

Invest KOREA's incorporation procedure lists seven steps for a foreign-invested company. Step five is the notification of incorporation and business registration at the tax office. The documents named for that step include the application form, articles of incorporation, the lease contract, the incorporation certificate, the shareholder list and the representative's passport.

In other words, a viewing schedule that starts after the company is incorporated can stall the registration. If you are still comparing sites, ask landlords or agents whether they will provide a lease in a form that can be submitted for business registration, and confirm this with the tax office.

Write the use requirements first

A brief that you can hand to an agent or landlord should cover the activity itself, not only the size of the space. A sensible outline:

  • What happens on site: office work, manufacturing, storage, teaching, customer visits, food preparation.
  • People: how many staff, and whether customers or students will be on the premises.
  • Equipment and deliveries: machinery, loading needs, storage.
  • Essentials versus preferences: separate what the space must have from what is merely nice to have.
  • Timing: when you need access, and how long you expect to stay.

The reason to be this specific is that permitted use is a legal question, not a matter of taste. Invest KOREA's procedure notes that authorisation or permission from competent authorities, such as a district office, a health center or the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety, is needed where it is deemed necessary for the business, and that the timeline varies by business type. A space that looks suitable may not be usable for your activity until the right authority has approved it.

Understand what protection a commercial tenant has

Korea has a Commercial Building Lease Protection Act. A law-firm article on the Korean Law Blog explains that, under the act, commercial leases of less than five years must be renewed if the tenant requests it, unless the landlord can show justifiable cause such as non-payment of rent. It also describes a cap on how much a landlord may raise the rent or deposit on renewal for tenants below a deposit threshold, and says "major tenants" with larger deposits fall outside some of these protections. The article also says the threshold is higher in Seoul and, in many other parts of Korea, significantly lower.

That article is an older general explanation, and the thresholds and renewal periods in this area have been amended before. Treat the details as a reason to ask your adviser two direct questions: does the act cover this particular lease, and what are the current renewal and rent-increase limits for this region and deposit level?

Consider zones if the business is industrial or technology-based

If you need factory or research space, an ordinary commercial lease may not be the best route. Invest KOREA's industrial sites guide describes foreign investment zones where land is leased long-term, with annual rent calculated at a small percentage of the land's acquisition value and possible reductions for qualifying investments. It states that complex-type zones require a minimum foreign ownership stake and registration as a foreign-invested company. These zones suit specific industries and investment levels, so they are an option to check early, not a fallback after a failed search.

A viewing checklist

  1. Bring the written use brief and ask whether the space can legally host that activity.
  2. Ask for the lease terms in writing, including the term, deposit, monthly rent and renewal provisions.
  3. Confirm that the lease can be used to register the business at the tax office.
  4. Ask which permits would be needed from the district office, health center or other authority, and whether the building's classification allows them.
  5. Ask who pays for utilities, maintenance and any restoration at the end of the lease.
  6. Have the contract reviewed by a professional who reads Korean before signing.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Choosing a space on appearance and location before checking permitted use.
  • Signing a lease that cannot be used for business registration.
  • Assuming tenant protections apply without checking the deposit level and region.
  • Relying on an English summary of the lease instead of the Korean contract text.
  • Describing the business vaguely, which makes it harder for an agent or adviser to flag problems.

Rules change: confirm with the official bodies

This article is general information, not legal or financial advice. Lease law, permit requirements and registration documents change and differ by location and business type. Confirm current requirements with the tax office, the relevant district office or permitting authority, and Invest KOREA/KOTRA, and consult a licensed lawyer before signing a lease.
