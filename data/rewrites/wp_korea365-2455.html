Housing is the largest and least predictable part of a Seoul budget, while public transport and health insurance are the parts with published official rules. One cost-of-living aggregator, Wise, lists an average of about USD 1,972 a month for one person in Seoul, or about USD 1,081 without rent. Those are aggregated estimates, so this article separates figures that come from official or news sources from figures that come from third-party estimates, and shows where each cost can be checked.

Transport: the fare increase is documented

According to the Korea Herald, Seoul Metro raised the adult subway base fare from 1,400 won to 1,550 won, with teenager fares moving from 800 to 900 won and child fares from 500 to 550 won. The newspaper noted that round-trip fares passed 3,000 won for the first time, and that Seoul Metro cited a cumulative deficit of nearly 19 trillion won. An earlier Korea Herald report described city bus fares rising to 1,500 won and distance charges that apply after 10 kilometers, at an additional 100 won for every 5 kilometers.

For regular riders, VisitKorea lists the Climate Card, an unlimited pass for Seoul, with short-term passes from 5,000 won for one day to 20,000 won for seven days and a 3,000 won physical card. The Korea Herald figures let you compare: ride count times the base fare against the pass price. Wise lists a monthly transport pass at about USD 44, which is an aggregated figure, so compare it with your own commute.

Health insurance: a required cost for many residents

The National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) states that employed foreigners are compulsorily enrolled, with premiums calculated as monthly wage times a contribution rate and split equally between worker and employer. Self-employed foreigners with more than six months of residence must enroll, and the premium is based on a contribution score with a minimum equal to the average local rate. Students and certain visa holders receive reductions of 30 to 70% depending on visa type and duration, according to NHIS. If you are budgeting as a student or a self-employed resident, ask NHIS for your own premium instead of relying on an average.

Housing: what third-party estimates show

For rent, the Wise page lists, for Seoul, a one-bedroom apartment in the city centre at about USD 891 a month and outside the centre at about USD 605, and a three-bedroom apartment at about USD 2,166 in the centre and USD 1,197 outside. KRinsider, a guide site that says it draws on public data sources, puts the average monthly rent for a standard Seoul apartment at KRW 1,515,000 in March 2026 and gives these typical ranges by housing type:

The same KRinsider page mentions monthly management fees of KRW 80,000 to 300,000, internet and TV at KRW 30,000 to 45,000 and winter gas bills of up to KRW 250,000; ask any landlord what is included in the management fee before comparing two flats.

Food and everyday spending

Wise lists about USD 477 a month for groceries per person, about USD 149 a month for dining out, a meal at an inexpensive restaurant at about USD 9 and basic utilities at about USD 159 a month, with internet around USD 19. These are averages, so a better method is to track your own first month of spending and compare it to these figures.

How to build your own Seoul budget

  1. Fix the housing line first. Get actual listings for your target district and note both the deposit and the monthly rent, plus the management fee.
  2. Add transport from your commute. Multiply daily rides by the current base fare, then compare with a pass.
  3. Add health insurance from NHIS. Use the category that matches your visa and work status.
  4. Estimate food from your own habits, then compare with the Wise averages for reasonableness.
  5. Add a buffer line for items that vary, such as heating in winter.
  6. Re-check each season. Fares and rents move.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Ignoring the deposit. Rental deposits are large lump sums, separate from monthly rent.
  • Using USD figures without converting at the current rate. The Wise numbers are in dollars; exchange rates move.
  • Forgetting management fees and heating. They can change the real monthly cost.
  • Assuming insurance is optional. NHIS states enrollment is compulsory for the groups it describes.
  • Treating one source as the market. Compare at least two listings or sources.

Prices and rules change: confirm with the official bodies

This article is general information, not financial, tax or legal advice. Transit fares are set by Seoul Metropolitan Government and Seoul Metro, insurance premiums by the National Health Insurance Service, and housing costs by the market and by lease contracts. Check each directly, and take advice before signing a lease or committing to a budget.
