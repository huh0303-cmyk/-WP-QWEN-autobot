To judge a K-beauty review, check it against what the product label itself says: the Korean label should show the product name, ingredient list, net content, expiry or batch information and precautions, and a review is only useful if it describes the same version of the product you are holding. Read the label first, then read reviews from people whose skin concerns match yours, and treat anything the review claims about "effects" as that person's experience rather than a tested result.

What a Korean cosmetic label must contain

According to ChemLinked's guide to Korean cosmetic regulation, labels must carry, in Korean, the product name, manufacturer and responsible seller information, a complete ingredient list, net volume or weight, batch number and expiry or use-by date, price, and precautions for use. It adds that products classed as functional cosmetics must be marked as such. Freyr Solutions, a regulatory consultancy, gives a similar list and says the ingredient list is in descending order, so ingredients present in larger amounts appear first.

These are descriptions from industry sources, not the regulation text itself, and they differ slightly in what they list. Use them to know what to look for on the box, and confirm the rules with the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) if a decision depends on them.

General cosmetic or functional cosmetic

ChemLinked explains that Korea separates general cosmetics from functional cosmetics. General cosmetics make no specific functional claim and need no pre-market approval, though they must meet safety and labeling standards. Functional cosmetics are limited to listed claim categories, including skin whitening, anti-wrinkle effects, UV protection, hair dyes and anti-hair loss, anti-acne for rinse-off products, and dry skin relief, and they require pre-market evaluation or reporting.

Freyr states that claims must be substantiated and that functional cosmetics face specific labeling and advertising rules. When a review praises a product for "brightening" or "wrinkle repair", look at whether the package carries the functional cosmetic marking. If it does not, the product is in the general category and the claim is the reviewer's impression.

How to read the ingredient list

  1. Find the full list on the outer box or the container. Online listings can be incomplete, so compare them with the physical label when you can.
  2. Note the first several ingredients, since the list runs from larger to smaller amounts according to Freyr.
  3. Find any ingredient you have reacted to before. A reviewer's skin may not react the same way as yours, so your own history counts for more than another reviewer's praise.
  4. Check whether the product is a different version of one you already use, such as a new formula or a limited set.
  5. Look for the expiry or batch information described above, especially on discounted stock.

The mayk-factory regulation overview says Korea bans about 1,030 ingredients from cosmetics and that cosmetics require notification to the MFDS before sale. That is a manufacturer-side check. It does not tell you whether a product suits your skin.

Reading the reviews themselves

Korean shopping apps and stores often let you filter or read feedback by reviewer details such as skin type. Where such filters exist, start with reviewers who match your skin type and your main concern, such as dryness or blemishes, rather than the overall star score. Then read for specifics.

  • Useful details: texture, scent, how long the person used it, what else was in their routine, and whether irritation appeared.
  • Weak signals: one-line praise, reviews written right after purchase, and reviews that mention a free gift or an event.
  • Mismatches: a review of a different size, set or version than the one you are buying.

Since reviews are individual experiences, one very positive review is no evidence that a product is safe or effective for you.

A short checklist for a shopping trip

  • Write down the product names you want, with the size and version from the review you trusted.
  • Read the label in store: ingredient list, net content, expiry or batch information, and the functional cosmetic mark if the review mentions a functional claim.
  • Compare the price per unit of volume using the net content on the label.
  • Keep the receipt and ask the store about its return policy for opened cosmetics.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Trusting the star score alone. It mixes skin types that may not resemble yours.
  • Believing a claim without checking the category. Only products marked as functional cosmetics are in the regulated claim categories.
  • Skipping the expiry or batch information. The label sources above list it as required information.
  • Assuming the online ingredient list matches the box. Compare them.
  • Treating a review as a medical opinion. If you have a skin condition or a known allergy, ask a dermatologist or pharmacist.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This article is general information about cosmetics, not medical or legal advice. Cosmetic labeling and claim rules are set by the Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) and can change, and the summaries above come from industry sources rather than the legal text. For allergies, skin conditions or a reaction to a product, speak with a health professional. For regulatory questions, check with the MFDS.
