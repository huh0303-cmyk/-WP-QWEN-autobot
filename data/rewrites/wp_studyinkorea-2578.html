Exchange study in Korea is becoming a larger part of the international student picture, but for you the practical questions are the same as ever: which visa category your stay falls under, what your host university needs from you, and how much money you must show. The Study in Korea portal lists a dedicated D-2-6 sub-type for exchange students, while one Korean embassy says stays under 90 days belong under D-2-8, so the category depends on how long you stay and on the arrangement between your home university and the Korean host. This article explains what the sources say about the broader trend and then walks through the steps that matter for an exchange applicant.

What the numbers and policy say about exchange study

According to Wikipedia's article on international students in South Korea, the number of international students rose from 160,165 at one earlier point it reports to 208,962 at its latest point. At the latest point, 69.8% were in degree programmes and 30.2% were non-degree students, a group the page describes as exchange or language students. It also says 56% attended universities in the Seoul Metropolitan Area.

The same page says the government announced a five-year plan to attract 300,000 international students, aiming to make Korea one of the top 10 study-abroad destinations by student count by 2027. It lists lifting Korean language requirements for admission, expanding internship and employment opportunities and a quick visa system among the components. The page adds that the government gives financial assistance to selected exchange students for four-month periods. Check each of these points on official sites before relying on them, because the page is a general reference.

Korea.net reports a related signal on the language side: TOPIK test takers passed 550,000 and the 2026 plan has 15 sessions, six paper-based, six internet-based and three speaking. That tells you demand for Korean-language credentials is growing, though an exchange place does not always require a TOPIK score.

Which visa fits an exchange student

The Study in Korea portal lists D-2-6 as the sub-type for exchange students within the D-2 study visa. The Korean Embassy in the UK says the D-2 visa covers regular courses including exchange, bachelor's, master's and doctoral programmes, and that applicants staying under 90 days should apply for D-2-8 instead.

The Korean consulate page for D-2-8 describes it as a visiting-student visa for students enrolled in a regular degree programme at an overseas university who want to complete coursework in Korea for up to 12 months, including credit-bearing summer or winter programmes of under 90 days. It lists a certificate of admission, the Korean university's business registration certificate, a passport valid for six months or more and a visa fee, and says the maximum stay is 12 months with a single entry at first.

Ask your host university's international office which category it will use for your semester, because the document list follows from that.

Money: what you may need to show

The embassy page in the UK says exchange students and those staying under 12 months must show funds that cover the whole cost of the stay in Korea. The LA consulate page says additional documents may be required, possibly including bank statements covering three months, depending on the university's category. Mason Korea's visa guide says the government requires proof of funds in the name of the applicant or the applicant's parent or legal guardian, and that a guardian's funds need family-relation documents such as birth certificates.

Different offices state different rules, so ask your host university which one applies and obtain bank statements only when asked for them.

A realistic application timeline

Mason Korea's page gives an example of how long things take at one campus. For the fall intake, applications open in the second week of June and close on the second Friday of July, and for the spring intake they open on the first day of December and close on the last Friday of January. It says the certificate of admission review takes two to three weeks and that embassy processing takes another two to three weeks. Other universities use other dates, but the sequence is typical: admission decision, certificate of admission, visa application at the embassy or consulate, then travel.

  1. Confirm with your home university that you are nominated for an exchange place.
  2. Apply to the Korean host university through its exchange application, with the documents it lists.
  3. Receive the certificate of admission and business registration certificate.
  4. Prepare your passport, photo, financial proof and fee for the visa application.
  5. Submit the visa application to the Korean embassy or consulate in the country you are in.
  6. After arrival, ask the host office about foreigner registration. Seoul National University's guide says registration is required within 90 days of arrival for D-2 holders, but check your own case.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Using the wrong visa category. Under 90 days and over 90 days can fall under different categories.
  • Applying too late. Two review stages of two to three weeks each can take more than a month.
  • Assuming the money rule from a blog. The rules for exchange students may differ from those for degree students.
  • Forgetting the passport validity. The consulate page requires at least six months.
  • Treating government plans as promises. The 300,000-student target and exchange assistance are policy aims; ask your university what applies to you.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This article is general information, not legal or immigration advice. Visa categories, fees, document lists and funding rules change, and each embassy and university applies them in its own way. Before you pay or book, confirm the current requirements with the Korean embassy or consulate that will handle your application, HiKorea, the Study in Korea portal and your host university's international office.
