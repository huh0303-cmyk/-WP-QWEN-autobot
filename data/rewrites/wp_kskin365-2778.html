Korea's Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) sets the rules for cosmetic safety, but it does not individually approve every skincare product before sale. Most everyday products are "general cosmetics" that must meet safety standards without pre-market registration, while products making specific claims such as whitening, anti-wrinkle or sun protection go through a stricter review as "functional cosmetics". Knowing that split helps you read what a Korean label does and does not tell you.

Two categories, two levels of review

According to ChemLinked's overview of Korean cosmetic regulation, MFDS oversees cosmetic safety management, rule-making and the evaluation of functional cosmetics. The same page says Korea divides cosmetics into general cosmetics, with no pre-market registration but a duty to comply with safety standards before distribution, and functional cosmetics, which face pre-market scrutiny for specific claims.

ChemLinked's page on functional cosmetics says the category covers skin whitening, anti-wrinkle and elasticity products, UV protection, hair dyeing and removal, hair-loss treatment, acne management (for rinse-off cleansers only), skin barrier restoration and stretch-mark appearance. A product with a new ingredient or formulation goes through full evaluation, whereas one that uses MFDS-notified functional ingredients at specified concentrations can follow a simplified report.

Artixio's regulatory guide makes the same point about functional products: those claiming whitening, anti-wrinkle, sunscreen or hair-loss benefits need MFDS review and approval before they can be sold.

Who is responsible for a product on the market

ChemLinked says distributors must register as responsible cosmetic sellers and employ a qualified manager who oversees quality control and post-market safety. Artixio describes the same local responsible entity, which must keep manufacturing records, run quality checks and ensure safety compliance. For imported products, Artixio adds that importers submit an entry notice to the Korea Pharmaceutical Trade Association before customs clearance. ChemLinked also lists good manufacturing practice (CGMP) standards and continuous collection of adverse-event data after launch.

ChemLinked adds that manufacturing and import volumes and ingredient lists are subject to annual reporting, and that imports require business registration and manufacturing-related certificates for customs clearance. Together these requirements mean the regulator can trace a product back to the company that placed it on the market, which is the main route for follow-up if a safety problem emerges. They are obligations on companies, not something shoppers need to file, but they explain why a legitimately imported product normally has a named Korean company on its packaging.

How ingredient limits work

ChemLinked's ingredient page describes a prohibited list that it puts at over 1,000 ingredients, along with restricted lists in four categories: preservatives, hair dye ingredients, UV filters and other controlled ingredients. Each restricted entry has permitted functions, maximum concentration and conditions of use. Colourants work on a positive list, with limits such as no use in the eye area or rinse-off products only.

Artixio calls this a negative-list system, in which ingredients are allowed unless restricted, with strict limits on heavy metals, harmful chemicals and microbial contamination. ChemLinked's summary adds a general ban on animal-tested cosmetics and ingredients, with a few narrow exceptions.

These pages are industry regulatory summaries rather than the ministry's own text, so check ingredient concerns against MFDS before acting on a specific number.

What a Korean label should show

ChemLinked says products must carry, in Korean, the product name, manufacturer address, a full ingredient list, net weight or volume, batch number and expiry information, plus precautions for use. Functional cosmetics must show their functional designation. Its ingredient page adds that 25 designated fragrance allergens must be listed individually if they exceed 0.01 percent in rinse-off products or 0.001 percent in leave-on products.

When you buy online from overseas, a missing Korean label does not by itself prove a product is unsafe, but it means you are relying on the seller's description. Photograph or save the product name, ingredient list and instructions in case you need to ask a pharmacist or doctor about a reaction.

A short shopping check

  1. Read the full ingredient list, including the fragrance allergens at the end.
  2. Look for the functional-cosmetic designation if the product claims whitening, anti-wrinkle or sun protection.
  3. Identify the responsible company and the manufacturer on the pack.
  4. Check the batch number and expiry information.
  5. Be cautious with products that make drug-like promises but show no functional designation.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Believing that MFDS "approved" a product. For general cosmetics, ChemLinked says there is no pre-market registration. Approval language usually applies to functional cosmetics.
  • Assuming "Korean" means regulated. A product may be made elsewhere or sold through a channel that skips local labelling.
  • Treating a missing ingredient from the prohibited list as proof of safety. Restricted ingredients can still cause reactions, and individual skin differs.
  • Ignoring symptoms. Persistent irritation is a reason to stop the product and speak to a healthcare professional.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This is general information, not medical advice. Ingredient lists, concentration limits and the functional-cosmetic categories are revised from time to time. For current rules, check the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety, and for a reaction or a health concern, speak to a pharmacist or doctor.
