Before a K-pop group releases anything, its members usually spend years as trainees: auditioning, training in several skills, being evaluated, and waiting to be chosen for a debut lineup. A KcontentHub guide puts the typical trainee period at two to five years, but Korean press reports show the path can run much longer, cost a great deal, and end without a debut. This article follows the pipeline from audition to teaser campaign and looks at what recent reporting says about its costs.

Auditions and selection

According to KcontentHub, agencies hold hundreds of open and global auditions each year, and tens of thousands of people apply annually in countries such as the US, Japan and China. Applicants are evaluated on vocals, dance, visuals, potential and personality. The guide is an industry explainer rather than an official source, so treat its numbers as descriptive.

What trainees study

The KcontentHub guide lists the curriculum as vocals, dance, foreign languages, acting, fitness and character building, and says trainees commonly work 8 to 12 hours a day, six days a week. The Korea Herald's profile of trainees paints a similar picture through individual accounts: lessons in vocals, dance and acting, physical training, weight checks, language classes for international trainees, and sessions from morning until late at night on top of school attendance. One former SM Entertainment trainee quoted there trained for five years without debuting; the group member Jo-kwon of 2AM trained with JYP Entertainment for seven years before his debut; and a former YG Entertainment trainee left after two years.

A Korea Times report on trainee numbers describes typical schedules as running from 9 a.m. to 10 p.m.

Evaluations, survival shows and debut lineups

Regular monthly or quarterly evaluations assess singing, dancing and stage presence, according to KcontentHub. It adds that survival shows such as Boys Planet bring in public voting, and that agencies pick debut teams on visuals, skills, chemistry, marketability and global appeal, with some companies, SM among them, using internal and fan-based voting to finalize lineups. The Korea Herald stresses the other side: agencies can drop trainees at any time, and a long contract is no guarantee of a debut.

The visual story before the music

Visual storytelling starts before debut. KcontentHub describes pre-debut teaser content, including videos, photos and behind-the-scenes material, released across YouTube, Instagram and other social media to build a fandom ahead of the official debut. The debut stage then includes TV music show performances and a debut showcase.

The music itself often arrives from outside the trainee system. The Korea Times reports that agencies run song camps where international composers produce demos, and Korean A&R teams select songs and add Korean lyrics. So a debut combines a Korean-trained group, a concept and visual campaign, and songs that may have been written internationally.

The cost and who can afford it

Two Korea Times articles give figures:

  • Trainee numbers fell to 1,170 in 2022 from 1,895 in 2020, a 38.3 percent decrease, and the voluntary quit rate rose to 34.4 percent from 31 percent.
  • JYP Entertainment spent 1.12 billion won ($765,000) a year on trainee development, up 30 percent from 850 million won, with estimated per-trainee monthly costs of about 3.12 million won.
  • Specialized weekend academy courses at major agencies cost about 720,000 won for a four-week session, excluding private lessons.

The articles argue that rising costs have changed who enters. One reports that wealthy families and second-generation entertainers are more visible among trainees, and quotes a mid-sized agency manager saying that middle-class and wealthier families have noticeably increased among recent trainees. It also reports that parents increasingly see the path as personal branding, and it cites Circle Chart data showing seven of the top ten most-streamed songs in the previous year came from major agencies.

Health, age and exit paths

The Korea Times article on declining trainee numbers reports several concerns: a claim that seven to eight out of ten female teenage trainees experience menstrual irregularities, trainees describing hyperventilation and heavy psychological stress, and trainees past 20 feeling considered too old. It also says trainees often lack alternative career preparation despite low odds of debut. These are reported claims from one outlet, not official statistics, but they explain why some families weigh the decision carefully.

If you are a fan, a parent or a student: practical checks

  1. Ask any agency or academy for written contract terms before signing, including who pays which costs.
  2. Find out what happens to your schooling and daily schedule.
  3. Ask what health support exists for trainees.
  4. Check what the agency says about the odds and the timeline for debut, and compare it with press reports.
  5. For fans: remember that a debut teaser is a marketing campaign, and read both the promotional material and independent reporting.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Assuming training leads to debut. The Korea Herald says only a fraction debut.
  • Treating one trainee's story as typical. Training length ranged from two to seven years in the cases quoted.
  • Quoting cost figures without noting they are estimates from a single agency's reported spending.
  • Confusing audition rumors with official announcements.

Practices and figures change

Training systems, contract practices and statistics differ between agencies and change over time. This article summarizes press reporting and an industry guide, not legal or career advice. Anyone considering an audition or contract should check the agency's official notices and take independent legal advice on contract terms.
