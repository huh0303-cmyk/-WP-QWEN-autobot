Foreign companies that offer AI-based products to people in Korea can fall under two separate laws: the AI Basic Act and the Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA). According to White & Case, the AI Basic Act entered into force on January 22, 2026 and reaches foreign operators whose systems affect Korean users or markets. PIPA, as summarized by Baker McKenzie, applies to foreign operators that provide goods or services to people in Korea or process Korean residents' personal data in ways that have a direct and substantial impact. This article explains how the two fit together and what an investor should check first.

Two laws, two different questions

The AI Basic Act asks how an AI system is built, disclosed and supervised. PIPA asks how personal data about identifiable people is collected, used, stored and sent abroad. A company that trains or runs an AI service on Korean user data usually has to answer both questions, and meeting one law does not settle the other.

White & Case reports that the Ministry of Science and ICT (MSIT) leads enforcement of the AI Basic Act, and that a Korean AI Safety Institute, still being set up at the time of its tracker, is expected to handle risk evaluation and certification. Personal data matters sit with the data protection regulator under PIPA. Confirm the current division of responsibilities with each body before you file or answer any inquiry.

When a foreign business is in scope

Baker McKenzie's summary of the PIPA guidelines for foreign business operators lists three situations in which the law applies:

  • The operator provides goods or services aimed at Korean data subjects. Factors such as language, currency, web domain and delivery methods are used to judge this.
  • The operator processes Korean residents' personal information with a direct and substantial impact, even without selling directly to them.
  • The operator has a Korean subsidiary that processes personal data as part of global operations.

For the AI Basic Act, White & Case and the IAPP both state that foreign companies must appoint a domestic representative if they meet any one of three thresholds: annual revenue above KRW 1 trillion, revenue from AI services above KRW 10 billion, or at least one million daily users in Korea. A small foreign start-up may therefore be outside the representative requirement while still being inside the Act's general reach, so reading the thresholds literally is the first step.

What the AI Basic Act asks for

The IAPP describes three regulatory tracks: transparency duties for generative and high-impact AI, safety requirements for frontier models, and governance duties for high-impact systems in sensitive domains. White & Case adds that enhanced oversight targets applications in areas such as energy, healthcare, nuclear facilities and biometric investigations.

On generative AI, White & Case says the Enforcement Decree requires operators to give users advance notice, with the form and content of that notice specified. It also reports a compute threshold of 1026 FLOPS above which operators face enhanced risk-management and user-protection measures. Most investors will not train models at that scale, but those who build on top of a model should ask their provider which obligations pass down to them.

The IAPP notes that operational detail is being filled in through presidential decree and ministry guidelines. That means a company should track the decree and guidelines, not only the statute text.

Personal data rules that sit underneath

Baker McKenzie highlights one rule that surprises many foreign teams: when personal data of Korean residents is transferred overseas, the operator must clearly disclose in writing the details of the processing, including the country and the name of the overseas entity involved. A privacy policy that only says data may be shared with affiliates is unlikely to meet that description. Check that your policy names the recipient and the country.

The same guidance says that where a foreign operator has a Korean presence and must appoint a domestic representative, it is desirable to designate the Korean entity. Baker McKenzie's summary does not give penalty amounts for PIPA, so ask counsel or the regulator for the current figures instead of assuming them.

Penalties and the grace period

White & Case reports administrative fines of up to KRW 30 million for failing to give required notification, failing to designate a representative, or refusing an inspection. It also reports that the authorities announced a one-year grace period before administrative fines apply. A grace period on fines is not an exemption from the duties themselves, so building compliance during that window is still the safer course. Verify the grace period's start and end dates with MSIT, because the sources read here do not give them.

A short checklist for investors

  1. Map each AI feature and note whether it uses Korean users' personal data, whether it generates content, and whether it touches a sensitive domain such as healthcare or biometrics.
  2. Compare your Korean revenue, AI revenue and daily Korean users with the three representative thresholds.
  3. Decide who in Korea would act as domestic representative, and write down the decision.
  4. Rewrite the privacy policy so overseas transfers name the country and the receiving entity.
  5. Draft the advance notice users would see for any generative feature.
  6. Assign one person to follow MSIT announcements on the decree and guidelines.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Assuming a foreign head office is outside the law. Both laws are described as reaching foreign operators that serve or affect people in Korea.
  • Treating the thresholds as a safe harbor. Being below the representative thresholds does not remove other duties.
  • Relying on a vendor's statement. If a third-party model powers your product, you still need to know what notice and risk duties apply to you.
  • Using secondary summaries as final authority. Law-firm trackers differ in detail and age. Read the current statute, decree and guidance.

Rules change: confirm with the official bodies

This article is general information, not legal advice. The decree, guidelines and enforcement practice are still developing. Before you make a compliance decision, confirm the current text and your own facts with the Ministry of Science and ICT for AI matters, with the Personal Information Protection Commission for data matters, and with a qualified Korean lawyer.
