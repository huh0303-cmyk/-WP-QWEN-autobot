Foreigners who live in or visit Korea generally cannot trade on the main Korean crypto exchanges, and the number who hold working accounts is tiny. According to a Korea Times report from August 2026, foreign nationals have effectively been shut out of domestic exchanges since a 2021 revision to the Act on Reporting and Using Specified Financial Transaction Information. Trading through a real-name bank account is technically allowed, but foreigners face barriers in getting one.

What the rule actually says

Korean exchanges operate on a real-name account system. Lightspark's summary of Korean crypto law says providers must use real-name bank accounts for transactions and carry out strict know-your-customer checks, and that virtual asset service providers must register with the Korea Financial Intelligence Unit.

The Korea Times describes the result for foreigners: although trading through real-name-verified bank accounts is technically permitted, foreigners find such accounts hard to obtain because of concerns about money laundering and illegal foreign exchange activity. The paper quotes an industry official saying Korea is the only country that prohibits legally resident foreigners from trading. Treat that as an industry view, not an official statement.

How few foreigners are active

The Korea Times gives figures as of August 2026. Foreign-held accounts totalled 566,352 across five major exchanges:

  • Bithumb: 484,846
  • Upbit: 42,449
  • Coinone: 15,914
  • DigitalX: 11,622
  • GOPAX: 11,521

Only 517 of these accounts (0.09%) had completed know-your-customer verification, and 90 (0.01%) were active with recent transactions. Foreign holders had KRW 2.58 billion in deposits and KRW 30.36 billion in cryptocurrencies. The report says no official regulatory change had been announced, though industry officials were pushing for more foreign participation. In other words, an account that exists on paper may not be usable for trading.

Protections that apply to users who can trade

For anyone with a working account, the Virtual Asset User Protection Act sets duties for the exchange. The Block reports that it took effect in July 2024 and requires exchanges to:

  • keep at least 80% of user crypto deposits in cold storage, separate from company funds;
  • have licensed local banks hold customers' cash deposits;
  • maintain crypto reserves matching customer deposits in amount and type;
  • carry insurance or set up reserve funds for security breaches; and
  • run real-time monitoring to detect and report suspicious trading.

CoinGeek adds that the Financial Services Commission oversees policy while the Financial Supervisory Service handles monitoring and enforcement, and that rule-breakers can face criminal punishment, penalty surcharges, corrective orders and business suspension. These duties fall on the exchange. They do not guarantee that you will not lose money from price moves.

What may change next

A second round of crypto legislation is under discussion. Crypto.news reports that a Digital Asset Framework Act would address digital asset issuance and distribution, stablecoins, service providers, and disclosure and compliance, that ten separate bills were pending in the National Assembly, and that the Financial Services Commission planned to work with lawmakers on a consolidated bill, with completion targeted within the year. Pending bills are proposals, not rules; nothing in them changes what you may do until a law is passed and takes effect. Whether foreign residents' access changes is not addressed in the sources above, so watch for official announcements instead of rumours.

What a foreign resident should check before trying

  1. Ask the exchange, in writing, whether it accepts your visa type and what identity documents it needs. Do not assume a sign-up screen means you can trade.
  2. Check that the exchange is registered with the Financial Intelligence Unit and covered by the user protection rules.
  3. Check whether you can open the real-name bank account the exchange requires.
  4. Confirm whether KYC verification is actually complete before depositing money.
  5. Ask about withdrawal limits and what happens to funds if verification lapses.

Tax and reporting: verify with the tax authority

Lightspark's summary states that profits from crypto trading are not currently subject to capital gains tax, and that planned taxation was postponed. That is a secondary source, and tax treatment of virtual assets is one of the areas most likely to change. Do not rely on it. Ask the National Tax Service, or a licensed tax professional, how gains, transfers and holdings are treated for your residency status, including any obligations in your home country.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Believing a large account count means foreigners can trade freely. The Korea Times figures show almost none are active.
  • Using an unregistered or overseas service without understanding that Korean user protections may not apply.
  • Using someone else's account or identity to get around verification. Real-name rules exist so that the account holder is the person trading.
  • Assuming the rules are fixed. The Korea Times notes that industry groups want changes, but no official change had been announced.
  • Relying on social media posts or exchange marketing instead of regulator notices.

Rules change: confirm with the official bodies

This article is general information, not legal, tax or financial advice, and it does not recommend buying any asset. Crypto rules in Korea are still developing, and the figures above are a snapshot from the sources named. Confirm current requirements with the Financial Services Commission, the Financial Intelligence Unit, the exchange you intend to use and the National Tax Service, and speak with a qualified professional before making decisions.
