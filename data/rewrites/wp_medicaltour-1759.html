The most useful medical consultation summary is a short, factual note that states what you want to ask, what records you have, and what practical arrangements you need. Send it through the provider's own channel, ask what additional records are required, and let the treating clinician decide what is relevant. Medical Korea, the official information platform for international patients, is a good place to start finding providers and confirming how the system works.

Start with an accredited or listed provider

Medical Korea's directory of most-visited hospitals lets you filter by region across 16 provinces and cities, by medical specialty with more than 20 options, and by interpreter availability across 14 languages. It listed 144 hospitals when this article was prepared, and each listing includes contact information, bed counts, interpreter services, meal options and accreditation status.

Medical Korea also describes a Korean accreditation program for institutions that attract international patients. It says the program certifies institutions through evaluation of service quality and patient safety, using 44 criteria for foreign patient management, including interpretation services, and 92 criteria for patient safety, including medication management and infection control. The site says the program is in its fourth cycle (2023-2026) and that eligible institutions must be hospital level or above with Korea Institute for Healthcare Accreditation certification. Accreditation is one signal among many; it does not tell you whether a particular treatment is right for you.

What to put in your consultation summary

Keep it to one page and use plain wording. Include:

  • Purpose. The service you are asking about and your main question.
  • Your preferred language. State what you need for communication, and ask whether an interpreter is available.
  • Relevant history. Conditions, past procedures and current medication, using the names exactly as written on your own records.
  • Records you hold. List reports, imaging and test results, and ask which formats the provider accepts.
  • Practical questions. Appointment length, what an estimate includes and what must be confirmed before you travel.
  • Timeline. The earliest and latest dates you could travel.

Do not try to translate technical terms from memory. Copy names from your documents and let the provider ask follow-up questions.

Handle records and privacy carefully

Share sensitive documents through the provider's stated secure route, not through public messages or open social media comments. Ask how the provider stores and uses what you send. If you work through an intermediary, ask who will see your records and whether they are passed on to the hospital.

Understand the visa step before you commit

Medical Korea's visa page describes two medical visa categories. C-3-3 is for short-term cases where treatment and travel take 90 days or less, and it gives simple procedures, including cosmetic procedures such as plastic surgery, as examples. G-1-10 is a long-term category allowing stays up to one year for treatment and rehabilitation needing longer recovery, with multiple entries during its one-year validity. The visa covers the patient and immediate family members assisting with care, including spouses and children.

According to the page, direct applicants submit a visa application and passport copy, medical diagnosis documentation, a document from a Korean medical institution confirming the appointment for treatment or recuperation, a financial affidavit showing ability to cover treatment costs, and proof of family relationship where applicable. Applications are made at Korean embassies or consulates, or through the medical institution using the HUNET online system, after which the patient presents the certificate to the consulate. Whether you need a visa at all depends on your nationality, so confirm with the embassy or consulate for your country.

A simple structure keeps the message readable: a two-line purpose statement at the top, a bullet list of records, then your questions. Keep personal identifiers to what the provider asks for, and avoid attaching documents until they confirm the secure upload or email route. If you are writing in a language the provider does not list, say so and ask which languages they handle, since the directory shows interpreter availability by language.

Questions to ask before you travel

  1. Is this a general information request, or a confirmed appointment?
  2. Is the estimate a final plan, or a range that depends on examination?
  3. Which records does the clinic need, and in what format?
  4. Is interpretation provided, and in which language?
  5. What follow-up care is available after the main visit, and how long should I plan to stay?
  6. What happens if the clinician advises a different plan after the consultation?

Questions about fitness to travel, recovery time and follow-up belong with the treating clinician, not with a booking agent. If you need urgent care, do not wait for an enquiry reply.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Treating an online estimate as a final price. Ask what it includes and when it can change.
  • Sending records over unsecured channels. Use the provider's secure route.
  • Booking travel before appointments are confirmed. Check cancellation terms first.
  • Assuming accreditation equals suitability. It reflects service and safety evaluation, not your individual case.
  • Overlooking the visa document list. The appointment confirmation from a Korean institution is one of the listed items.
