Young job seekers in Korea can use three kinds of government-backed help: the Work24 portal's youth programmes and job search, public-private schemes in which companies offer work experience and training, and job fairs held by the Ministry of Employment and Labor with local governments and companies. This article describes what the ministry and Work24 pages say about each and how to check whether a programme fits you. Eligibility rules differ by programme, so the last step is always to read the programme's own page.

Start with the youth menu on Work24

Work24 is the job portal run by the Ministry of Employment and Labor and the Korea Employment Information Service. Its main menu has a youth section whose items include a youth job-competency programme, a youth challenge support project, a youth growth project, a job-adaptation support service, a school-based job support office, a youth job leap incentive, and a work-experience programme. The portal also lists a national employment support system with separate Type I and Type II application pages for allowances.

The portal's employment policy list gives short descriptions of some of these. For example, the "Youth Growth Project - Youth Café" is described as providing psychological counselling and tailored programmes to support the motivation to look for work and a regular daily routine. A job-seeker support service is described as covering job recommendations and accompanied interviews. The National Tomorrow Learning Card is listed for vocational training, with a description saying that training in fields such as metal, machinery, electrical and electronics is supported at 90 percent or more of the cost. These are summaries from the list page, so open each programme page for conditions, age limits and application dates.

Company work-experience schemes

A Ministry of Employment and Labor news release describes the Youth "Leap" Membership, a public-private partnership in which participating companies use their own resources to give young people work experience, internships and training, with government support for operation and recruitment promotion. The release says the membership grew from 11 companies at its launch, including Samsung Electronics, POSCO, SK hynix and the Lotte Group, to 140 companies after new members joined. It gives two company examples: LG Uplus supports an IT training programme called the Eureka Software Training Program, and Kakao runs the Kakao Tech Campus, which offers team projects led by instructors with industry experience.

The practical point is that "work experience" in these schemes means structured programmes run by employers, not only a standard internship listing. If a company you like is a member, check its own recruitment page for such a programme.

Job fairs with on-site support

The ministry also reports on regional job fairs. In one release, a fair in Daejeon was described as the tenth and last in a series of region-specific job fairs held in cooperation with local governments and companies, with about 200 small and medium-sized enterprises running recruitment booths. On-site services included career and résumé consulting, a mock interview zone with video recording and feedback, and job experience booths. A bank partner offered a small interview support payment and a hiring subsidy to employers, according to the release.

A later release describes a joint declaration by the government and five major business organisations on youth employment. Among its commitments, the parties agreed to expand public-private joint job fairs to regional areas in 2026, expand company recruitment briefings in cooperation with regional universities, and identify more work experience and training programmes involving large companies, mid-sized companies and small and medium-sized enterprises. Commitments like these are plans, not guarantees, so look for actual event notices on Work24 and the ministry's site.

A practical sequence for a young applicant

  1. Create a profile on Work24 and look at the youth programme list.
  2. Note the eligibility line of each programme: age, status and residence conditions.
  3. Choose one programme for skills, such as training, and one for access, such as a job fair or a company scheme.
  4. Use the portal's résumé and cover-letter guides to prepare documents.
  5. Track application windows, since several programmes are limited by date.

If you are a foreign resident rather than a Korean citizen, the pages read for this article do not say which programmes are open to you. Ask the programme operator or a local Employment and Welfare Plus Center before you apply, and check the work permissions attached to your stay status.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Assuming every programme is open to everyone. Each has its own eligibility conditions.
  • Treating announcements as vacancies. A declaration or a plan is not a job opening.
  • Missing application windows. Check dates on the programme page.
  • Relying on summaries. List pages give one-line descriptions only.
  • Skipping local support. The ministry promotes Employment and Welfare Plus Centers as a place to get tailored employment support.

Rules change: confirm with the official bodies

Programme names, budgets and eligibility change from year to year. This is general information, not legal, financial or immigration advice. Confirm current conditions on Work24, with the Ministry of Employment and Labor (its counselling line is 1350 for labour and employment system questions, according to Work24) or at a local employment centre.
