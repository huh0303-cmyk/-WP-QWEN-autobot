Korea has two official resources that both go by the name Visa Navigator, and they do different jobs. One is an interactive tool on the visa.go.kr portal that suggests visa types from a few inputs. The other is a Korea Immigration Service guide, "Visa Navigator: Customized Stay Guide for Foreign Residents," that explains visa categories, employment limits and status changes. Both are starting points. Neither approves anything, and both send you to HiKorea or the Immigration Contact Center for case-specific requirements.

Tool one: the interactive navigator on visa.go.kr

The Korea Visa Portal includes a Visa Navigator that asks for your continent, your country or region, your purpose of entry and your length of stay. Purposes include tourism, treatment, study, professional employment and investment, and duration choices are 90 days or less, 91 days or more, or permanent residency. It then matches those answers to suggested visa types.

The same portal offers an individual e-visa service and electronic application forms, a fees section and a menu for required-document guidance for visa applications. It lists the general inquiry line 1345, which it calls the Foreign Resident Comprehensive Information Center. The landing page does not show specific fee amounts, so look them up in the fees section for your visa type.

Use this tool when you are outside Korea or planning a move and need a shortlist: for example, a student comparing study categories or a professional checking which employment status might fit.

Tool two: the Visa Navigator guide for people staying in Korea

The Ministry of Justice guide describes itself as a stay guide for foreigners residing or intending to stay in Korea. The English edition used for this article is labeled May 2023, so use it for structure and concepts and check HiKorea for current details. It covers:

  • Visa categories, grouped by type: diplomatic and official, visa-exempt and tourist, short-term stays up to 90 days, education and investment (including students D-2 and D-4 and job seekers D-10), professional and non-professional work (E-1 to E-10), family-based statuses and working holiday programs.
  • Employment scope for each status.
  • Flow charts for changing status.
  • Reporting obligations and examples of violations.
  • Application channels.

What the guide says about working

The employment section is the part many readers need. According to the guide, students on D-2 and D-4 generally cannot work, but may do limited part-time work after six months of residency in certain fields. Professionals on E-1 to E-7 may work within specified occupational boundaries. Statuses E-8 to E-10 are restricted to assigned sectors. Family statuses such as F-1 and F-3 do not allow employment and profit-making activities except in rare cases, while F-5 permanent residents face no restrictions. Anyone whose planned work falls outside their current status must meet the requirements and obtain permission for activities outside the status of stay.

The guide also shows how statuses can connect over time. One flow chart runs from student status to a professional status, then to resident and permanent resident, and another runs from job seeker to professional or startup status. Reading these charts early helps you see whether a status you are considering leads anywhere you want to go, before you commit to the application.

How to use the results step by step

  1. Write your facts down first. Nationality, current status, passport expiry, purpose and intended length of stay.
  2. Run the portal tool. Note every visa type it suggests, not only the first.
  3. Read the matching sections of the guide. Check who may work, what status changes are possible and which obligations apply.
  4. Open the required-document guidance. The portal has a menu for visa application documents.
  5. Contact an official channel. Ask HiKorea or 1345 to confirm which category applies to you.
  6. Choose your application route. The guide lists HiKorea e-application, authorized agents, a fax service for limited declarations, the visa.go.kr e-visa for eligible professionals and students, and Government24 for certificates. It says in-person visits require an online appointment booked on HiKorea.

The limits you need to respect

The guide states that, for specific information such as required documents, you should refer to the Immigration and Stay Guide on HiKorea or call the Immigration Contact Center on 1345. In practical terms:

  • A suggested visa type is not an eligibility decision.
  • The tool cannot see your documents, history or employer, so it cannot judge them.
  • Rules differ by nationality, and the tool only asks for a few inputs.
  • A guide edition from an earlier date may not reflect the latest fees, documents or procedures.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Treating the navigator as an approval. It only suggests categories.
  • Mixing up the two tools. One suggests visas, the other explains rules for residents.
  • Skipping the required-documents step. Confirm the document list for your exact category.
  • Forgetting reporting duties after arrival. The guide lists obligations such as foreign resident registration and reporting a change of address or workplace, each with a deadline in that edition, so confirm current deadlines.
  • Assuming an old fee still applies. The portal has a separate fees section.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This is general information, not legal or immigration advice. Visa categories, fees and documents change, and eligibility depends on your own circumstances. Confirm everything with HiKorea, the visa.go.kr portal, the Korean embassy or consulate that will handle your case, or the Immigration Contact Center on 1345. For complicated cases, speak with a qualified immigration professional.
