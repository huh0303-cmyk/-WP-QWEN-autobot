Korea's three big battery makers, LG Energy Solution, Samsung SDI and SK On, are leaning on energy storage systems (ESS) to offset slower electric vehicle demand. According to the Korea JoongAng Daily, all three posted quarterly operating profits in the April-to-June period for the first time in nearly two years. The Korea Times reports that they are also expanding ESS production in the United States. This article walks through the figures reported, where the capacity is going, and what an investor should check before drawing conclusions.

What the April-to-June results showed

The JoongAng Daily reported these second-quarter figures:

CompanyRevenueOperating profit
LG Energy Solution7.56 trillion won113.3 billion won
Samsung SDI3.77 trillion won203.8 billion won
SK On2.95 trillion won821.8 billion won

The paper described SK On's result as its first return to profit in nearly two years. It attributed the improvement to storage demand tied to AI data center expansion and renewable energy projects in North America, which offset the continuing slowdown in EV demand. A single quarter is a thin base for a trend, and the article does not break down how much of each profit came from ESS, so do not assume the whole improvement is storage-driven.

Why storage demand is rising

The JoongAng Daily links ESS growth to data centers and renewables. It also cites LG Energy Solution's expectation that its third-quarter ESS shipments would rise more than 50 percent from the April-to-June period. For context, the same article says Tesla's ESS deployments climbed 53 percent quarter over quarter while its energy business gross margin fell from 39.5% to 20.4%. That contrast is a useful warning: volume growth and margin growth are different things.

The Korea Times adds a policy factor. It says the U.S. "One Big Beautiful Bill Act" restricts Chinese battery components in tax-credit-eligible projects, which it presents as a tailwind for non-Chinese suppliers. Policy of this kind can change, so treat it as a risk factor as well as a driver.

Where capacity is being converted

According to the JoongAng Daily, the companies are converting U.S. EV battery lines to make ESS batteries at plants in Michigan, Tennessee, Indiana and Georgia. The Korea Times gives company-level targets:

  • LG Energy Solution: five ESS manufacturing sites in North America, expanding to more than 60 GWh of capacity by the end of the year, and a USD 1.6 billion LFP battery supply agreement with DTE Energy. It is described as the first Korean maker to mass-produce LFP batteries for ESS in the United States.
  • Samsung SDI: a target of 30 GWh of annual ESS capacity by the end of the year, with a joint venture plant in Indiana. The article calls it the only non-Chinese maker of prismatic ESS batteries in North America.
  • SK On: aiming for more than 20 GWh in global ESS orders, with its GRIDON platform planned for mass production in the third quarter of the following year.

The Korea Times also cites projections for the U.S. ESS market of 51 GWh in the base year it uses, 485 GWh in 2030 and 976 GWh in 2035. These are forecasts quoted by the newspaper, and forecasts of this length often miss.

What the margins suggest

Simple division of the reported figures shows how different the three results are. LG Energy Solution's operating profit of 113.3 billion won on revenue of 7.56 trillion won is roughly a 1.5% operating margin. Samsung SDI's 203.8 billion won on 3.77 trillion won is roughly 5.4%. SK On's 821.8 billion won on 2.95 trillion won is far higher, about 28%, which is unusual for a battery maker. The JoongAng Daily article as read here does not explain that gap, so check SK On's filing for one-off gains or accounting items before treating the figure as a run rate.

The lesson is that "returned to profit" covers very different levels of profitability. Compare margins and the reasons behind them, not just the headline.

What to read in company filings

News summaries are a starting point. For an investment decision, read each company's own disclosures and look for:

  1. ESS share of revenue and orders, stated separately from EV.
  2. Utilization rates on converted lines, and the cost of conversion.
  3. Whether announced capacity is installed, under construction or only planned.
  4. The terms of large supply contracts, including duration and price adjustment.
  5. Exposure to U.S. policy on Chinese components and tax credits.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Reading one profitable quarter as a turnaround. The sources report one quarter of results.
  • Mixing up capacity targets and shipments. A target in GWh is not revenue.
  • Treating a headline contract as the whole business. Check its size relative to the company.
  • Ignoring currency. Figures in won and dollars are not directly comparable without a stated exchange rate.

Rules and markets change: verify before acting

This article is general information, not investment, legal or tax advice, and it does not recommend buying or selling any security. Company results, contracts and U.S. policy can change quickly. Confirm figures in the companies' own filings and exchange disclosures, and speak to a licensed adviser before investing.
