The first job of personal finance is a cash reserve that covers essential spending for a few months, kept somewhere safe and easy to reach. After that come written goals with amounts and dates, and only then riskier investments. This article explains how to size an emergency fund, why the type of account matters in Korea, and how a few verified numbers can steer what you do next.

How much to keep as an emergency fund

The CFP Board's consumer site advises keeping at least three to six months of essential expenses in an emergency fund, or more if your job is at risk. Experian's overview notes that many financial firms use the same three-to-six-month rule of thumb, while some advisers argue for larger reserves, such as eight to twelve months or six to twelve months. It lists four factors that should change your target: your monthly spending, how stable your income is, how well your insurance covers you, and your investment and debt obligations.

To turn that into a number:

  1. Add up the essentials you must pay each month: rent, utilities, food, transport, insurance, minimum loan payments.
  2. Multiply by three for a stable salary, or toward six or more if your income is irregular, you are self-employed, or your job is uncertain.
  3. Subtract what you already hold in cash.
  4. Divide the gap by the number of months you give yourself to close it, and set up an automatic monthly transfer.

Experian's overview suggests practical ways to build the fund: automatic deposits, trimming expenses and savings challenges that add small amounts regularly. The easiest to sustain is a standing transfer on payday, so the money moves before you can spend it. Start with whatever amount you can maintain, even if it is smaller than the target.

What counts as an emergency

The CFP Board lists home or car repairs, insurance deductibles, loss of income from job loss, medical bills and unexpected expenses that need immediate funds. A holiday, a sale or a planned purchase is not an emergency. Writing this rule down helps you resist dipping into the fund.

Where to keep it: safe, liquid and covered

The CFP Board says the fund should be in a safe and liquid account that is easily accessible. In Korea, the Korea Deposit Insurance Corporation (KDIC) explains what is protected if a financial institution fails. Its English page states a protection limit of KRW 100 million per depositor, including principal and designated interest, calculated per institution rather than per deposit type or branch. Covered examples include demand deposits, time and savings deposits, installment deposits and foreign currency deposits.

KDIC's list of examples that are not covered includes certificates of deposit, mutual funds and money market funds, bank-issued bonds, repurchase agreements and beneficiary certificates. It also notes that if a depositor of a failed institution owes money to that institution, the debt is deducted from the deposit before protection applies. Confirm whether your own product is covered with the bank or on the KDIC website before you place your reserve there.

Set goals that have amounts and dates

A goal such as "save more" is hard to track. Write each goal with an amount, a deadline and the monthly sum it needs: for example, a target for a housing deposit, a training course, or retirement contributions. Sort them into short-term goals, which you can meet within a year or two, and long-term goals. Fund the emergency reserve first, then rank the others by urgency. Review the list when your income or family situation changes.

Debt and savings: compare the rates you pay and earn

When you have both debt and savings, compare the rate on each. The Seoul Economic Daily, citing Bank of Korea data for August, reports a fixed mortgage rate of 4.88 percent and deposit rates of 3.21 percent. Where a loan costs more than your savings earn, extra repayment can be worth considering after your emergency fund is in place, but check your loan contract for any early repayment charge first. The Bank of Korea raised its base rate to 3.00 percent on August 27, 2026, so rates on both sides are moving, and these numbers will change.

Cautious investing

Investments outside insured deposits are not protected by the deposit limit, as the KDIC exclusion list for funds and bonds shows. Before putting money in any product, ask the seller what it is, how it makes money, what it costs, and when you can withdraw. Do not invest money you may need for the emergency reserve or for a goal due within a year or two. Spread your holdings so that one product failing does not undo your plan.

Common mistakes to verify

  • Putting the emergency fund in a product that is not covered by deposit insurance or cannot be cashed quickly.
  • Keeping more than KRW 100 million at one institution without checking how the limit applies to you.
  • Forgetting the set-off rule when you also borrow from the same institution.
  • Using the fund for planned spending, then having nothing when a real emergency arrives.
  • Copying someone else's reserve target without looking at your own income stability.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This article is general information for educational purposes, not financial, tax or investment advice. Deposit protection limits, interest rates and product terms change. Confirm current details with KDIC, the Financial Supervisory Service and your bank, and consult a qualified financial planner for your own situation.
