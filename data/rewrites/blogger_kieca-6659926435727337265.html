The way to choose between study-in-Korea programs is to compare them on the same few criteria: what the program pays for, who is allowed to apply, how many selection stages there are, and which visa and registration steps follow. This article applies those criteria to two common routes, a university exchange and the Global Korea Scholarship (GKS), using published announcements and news reports.

Start with the objective

An exchange semester and a government-funded degree are different goals. An exchange is arranged between your home university and a partner, so it works only if your school has an agreement with a Korean university. The checklist published by the Korean visa application centre in Mongolia, for example, asks for a copy of the exchange agreement between the two institutions. A scholarship such as the GKS is a full program of study. Decide first whether you want a semester abroad that counts toward your current degree or a whole degree in Korea.

Criterion 1: what is paid for

The Korea JoongAng Daily describes the GKS undergraduate scholarship as a full-ride scholarship that also offers a monthly stipend, language study support and round-trip airfare. Yocket, an education information site, lists amounts such as a monthly allowance of roughly KRW 900,000 to 1,000,000 and one year of funded Korean language training, but figures are updated between application cycles, so rely on the current official announcement. For an exchange, ask your home university and the host what tuition you pay, who covers housing and what the sponsor letter must show. The Mongolia checklist requires a sponsor's letter covering expenses, with identification and income proof.

Criterion 2: who can apply

According to the Korea JoongAng Daily, for the undergraduate program applicants must be foreign nationals from countries designated by the National Institute for International Education (NIIED), be under 25 as of March 1 of the intake year, and have graduated or be expected to graduate from high school or an associate degree program. Yocket adds a grade rule, 80 percent or higher on a 100-point scale or top 20 percent of the class, along with a limit of under 40 for graduate programs and a ban on previous GKS recipients. Each yearly announcement sets its own rules, so check the one you are applying under.

Criterion 3: stages and deadlines

A Ministry of Foreign Affairs announcement for one embassy describes a three-round process: document screening and interviews at the embassy, then evaluation by NIIED, then review by universities, followed by the final announcement. It says the application is made through the Study in Korea website. The JoongAng Daily reports separate embassy and university tracks with different deadlines. Because deadlines move with each cycle, work backwards from the current notice and allow time to collect transcripts and certificates.

Criterion 4: how you must choose universities

The same embassy announcement says applicants on the general track may choose up to three universities, at least one of which must be a "Type B" university, while the R-GKS track allows up to two universities, both Type B. It also states that multiple applications result in disqualification and that applicants who submit a certificate of expected graduation must provide the official graduation document and transcripts by the stated date or lose the offer. Read the announcement's definitions, because the university types and tracks are explained there, not here.

Criterion 5: what comes after the program

Look at the visa and the career path before you apply. The Korea Immigration Service's Visa Navigator describes the D-10 job-seeker status that graduates can use to search for work, and the Korea Herald reports that the Study in Korea portal has become a permanent hub covering programs, scholarships, visas and employment. Korea.net reports that the K-Work platform helps international students find jobs and convert a D-2 or D-10 visa to an E-7-1 work visa. If staying to work matters to you, ask each program how it supports that step.

Putting the criteria together

Write the five criteria down the side of a page and fill in one column for each program you are considering. Mark each cell with the source you used, such as an official announcement, a university guide or a news report, and the date you read it. Cells you can only fill from a third-party site should be treated as unconfirmed until you check the official notice. If two programs tie on money and eligibility, the deciding factors are usually the timetable and what happens after you finish.

Common mistakes to verify

  • Using last year's scholarship age, grade or deadline rules.
  • Applying through more than one channel for the same scholarship, which the announcement says can disqualify you.
  • Not checking whether credits from an exchange will count at home.
  • Choosing universities without reading the type rules.
  • Relying on a third-party site's amounts instead of the official notice.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This article is general information and not immigration, legal or financial advice. Scholarship rules, benefits, deadlines and visa requirements change with every cycle. Confirm the current notice with the Study in Korea website, NIIED, your nearest Korean embassy or consulate, and the international office of the universities you are considering.
