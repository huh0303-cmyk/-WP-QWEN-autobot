Borrowing to buy Korean stocks has reached record levels. According to Seoul Economic Daily, citing the Korea Financial Investment Association, margin debt peaked at 38.6328 trillion won on June 24, up from 27.29 trillion won at the end of the previous year. The risk with margin debt is mechanical: when prices fall, lenders force sales. This article explains the numbers, what brokers did in response, and how recent governance changes relate.

How large the margin debt got

Seoul Economic Daily reports a record 38.4787 trillion won on June 19 and a higher peak of 38.6328 trillion won on June 24, an increase of about 10.5 trillion won over six months. KOSPI margin loans were 29.3296 trillion won, up about 70% from the year-end level, while KOSDAQ margin loans were 8.4319 trillion won, down from 10.1603 trillion won.

The Korea Herald gives a snapshot as of June 19: 38 trillion won, or USD 24.8 billion, with 28.9 trillion won on the KOSPI. It says borrowing against Samsung Electronics was 4.76 trillion won and against SK hynix 4.33 trillion won, a combined 9.1 trillion won. Borrowing on Samsung Electronics nearly tripled and on SK hynix rose fivefold since year-end. The figures differ slightly between the two articles because they refer to different dates.

What forced selling looks like

Seoul Economic Daily reports that forced liquidation, meaning sales triggered when a borrower cannot meet a margin call, reached 50.9 billion won on June 26, the fourth straight day above 40 billion won, and 271.7 billion won over the week of June 22 to 26. The Korea Herald says liquidations exceeded 550 billion won from June 1 to 9 and peaked at 169.8 billion won on June 9. Ultra-short-term brokerage receivables, which are another form of credit to investors, rose from 897.2 billion won at year-end to a peak of 2.0688 trillion won on June 25.

The Korea Capital Market Institute, quoted by the Herald, put the mechanism this way: margin trading boosts buying during rallies but increases selling pressure through margin calls in downturns.

How brokers responded

The Herald reports three examples. KB Securities restricted margin-financed buy orders. Meritz Securities raised the margin requirement to 100% on three stocks. Mirae Asset Securities reclassified 10 stocks between its internal stock groups. These are company decisions, so they differ by firm. If you use credit, read your own broker's current rules, because limits can change without notice.

Governance reform: a separate story

Margin debt shows how retail investors are positioned. Governance reform is about how companies treat shareholders. One law-industry summary, published by Global Law Experts, describes Commercial Act amendments that took effect on September 10, with these points: directors owe a duty of loyalty to the company and its shareholders as a whole; cumulative voting becomes the default for director elections at large listed companies; newly acquired treasury shares must be cancelled within one year; and thresholds for derivative suits, inspection rights and calling extraordinary meetings are lowered. That source is a secondary summary, so confirm the final text and which companies are covered with the Ministry of Justice or a Korean lawyer.

AMRO adds a related data point: 171 firms, about 6 percent of listed companies, had joined the Value-up program by the previous year-end. Governance reform is moving, but it covers a minority of companies.

Reading the margin data correctly

Two details in the reports are easy to miss. First, KOSPI and KOSDAQ moved in opposite directions: KOSPI margin loans were up about 70% from the year-end level, while KOSDAQ margin loans fell from 10.1603 trillion won to 8.4319 trillion won. Simple division of the Seoul Economic Daily figures shows KOSPI loans were roughly three quarters of the total, so the headline is mostly a story about large caps. Second, the record is a balance, not a flow. A balance can fall quickly when forced sales repay loans, which is why the liquidation figures matter as much as the peak.

The Korea Herald notes the data come from the Korea Financial Investment Association, which publishes them regularly, so you can check updated numbers yourself instead of relying on a news summary.

A checklist if you use credit to invest

  1. Ask your broker for the current margin requirement for each stock you hold.
  2. Calculate how far a stock can fall before a margin call, and keep cash for it.
  3. Avoid concentrating borrowed money in one or two stocks. The Herald figures show borrowing was heavily concentrated in two.
  4. Check whether your broker has restricted credit on stocks you own.
  5. Know the repayment date and interest cost before you borrow.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Comparing figures from different dates. Record highs moved by the day in the reports.
  • Assuming a stock's broker classification is permanent. Brokers change it.
  • Treating reform headlines as company-level facts. Check each company's own announcements.
  • Relying on a single summary of a law. Read the official text.

Markets and laws change: verify before acting

This is general information, not investment, tax or legal advice, and it does not recommend borrowing or buying anything. Confirm margin data with the Korea Financial Investment Association, rules with your broker, and legal changes with the Ministry of Justice or a qualified lawyer. Talk to a licensed adviser before using leverage.
