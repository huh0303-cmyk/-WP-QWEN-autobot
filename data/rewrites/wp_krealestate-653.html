Renting an apartment in Korea comes down to choosing a deposit structure, checking the property's documents before you pay anything, and then completing the registrations that protect your deposit after you move in. The two main structures are jeonse, a large refundable deposit with no monthly rent, and wolse, a smaller deposit plus monthly rent. The checks and filings below apply to both.

Jeonse, wolse and the options in between

According to Wikipedia's article on jeonse, a jeonse tenant pays a lump-sum deposit, described as anywhere from 50 to 80 percent of the market value of the property, and lives without monthly rent for the term, typically two years. In wolse, the same article says, the tenant pays a deposit of roughly 10 percent of the property value and then pays rent each month. A hybrid called banjeonse combines a reduced jeonse deposit with smaller monthly payments. These percentages are general descriptions, and actual deposits vary widely by building and area, so treat them as orientation rather than quotes.

SeoulStart's explainer on jeonse says the choice shifts risk: jeonse moves housing cost from monthly cash flow to deposit risk, because the landlord must return a large sum at the end. Wolse needs less savings upfront, which makes it the lower-risk option if you cannot afford to have a large deposit tied up.

What the Housing Lease Protection Act means for term and renewal

SeoulStart's summary of the Housing Lease Protection Act says a lease with a term shorter than two years is treated as two years. It also describes a statutory renewal right, often called "2+2", and says that for contracts signed after December 10, 2020 the landlord must give notice between six and two months before the lease ends. The same page gives a 5 percent cap on rent increases unless local ordinances set a lower one. Check these points against the law text or with a legal aid office before relying on them, because they affect when you can leave and what you may be asked to pay.

Check the documents before you pay

SeoulStart's guides to deposit scams and to the first month in Korea list the same verification steps, which cite Korean government guidance:

  1. Property registry (등기부등본): confirm the registered owner matches the person signing, and look for mortgages, seizures or other claims ranking ahead of yours.
  2. Building register (건축물대장): confirm the unit details and legal use, which matters especially for officetels and converted spaces.
  3. Senior claims and taxes: ask for written information on earlier deposits and unpaid taxes. Wikipedia notes that government tax claims rank ahead of tenant deposits when a property is auctioned.
  4. Payment account: transfer only to the landlord's account named in the contract.
  5. Representatives: if someone signs for the landlord, check their authorisation.

The scam guide describes "gap speculation", where a landlord uses tenants' deposits to fund a purchase when sale and jeonse prices are close. If the deposit is close to the sale price, take the registry checks especially seriously.

Housing type changes what you must ask

SeoulStart's housing-type guide says an officetel is classified as quasi-housing under the Housing Act but as a business facility under the Building Act, so you should confirm in writing that residential use and address reporting are permitted. For villa-type buildings, it says to find out whether the building is multi-household (다세대) or multi-family (다가구); in a 다가구 building you should investigate existing tenants' deposits and registered rights. It lists goshiwon as the lowest-commitment option for a temporary stay.

After you move in: protect the deposit

This is where renters most often lose protection by missing a step.

  • Address report: SeoulStart says that for foreign residents, foreigner registration and place-of-stay change reports stand in for resident registration, and that a registered foreigner must report a new place of stay within 15 days after moving, citing the Immigration Act.
  • Confirmed date (확정일자): the same guide says that once you have taken possession, completed the address report and obtained a confirmed date, priority to the deposit can arise from 0:00 the next day.
  • Lease report: it says covered leases with a deposit above 60 million won or monthly rent above 300,000 won must be reported within 30 days in listed regions, and that the filing can also establish the confirmed date.

Broker fees

SeoulStart explains that brokerage commission follows the Licensed Real Estate Agents Act schedule and cannot exceed the legal limit. For monthly-rent leases, the transaction amount used for the calculation is the deposit plus the monthly rent multiplied by 100. Ask the agent to show the calculation.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Paying a deposit or "holding fee" before reading the registry.
  • Assuming an officetel or converted unit allows address registration.
  • Missing the 15-day address report or the confirmed date.
  • Transferring money to an account in a name other than the landlord's.
  • Throwing away the contract, receipts or messages. SeoulStart recommends keeping the contract, registry copies, building register, transfer receipts, photos and agent communications.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This is general information, not legal or financial advice. Lease law, reporting thresholds and fees change, and individual contracts differ. Confirm the current rules with the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport, the court registry, your local district office or a licensed real estate agent, and consider free legal consultation from the Korea Legal Aid Corporation, which SeoulStart names as a resource, before signing a large-deposit lease.
