Metabolic health means your body handles energy well, which in practice shows up as waist size, blood pressure, blood sugar and blood fats that stay in a healthy range. When several of those drift out of range together, doctors call it metabolic syndrome. According to Johns Hopkins Medicine and the Cleveland Clinic, a diagnosis needs at least three of five markers, and the Cleveland Clinic says the condition can be reversed through lifestyle changes, with medication added when needed.

The five markers doctors check

Johns Hopkins Medicine describes metabolic syndrome as a cluster of risk factors for cardiovascular disease that raises the likelihood of diabetes, heart disease and stroke. You meet the definition with three or more of these:

  • Waist circumference: above 40 inches for men or 35 inches for women in the US-based criteria.
  • Triglycerides: 150 mg/dL or higher.
  • HDL cholesterol: below 40 mg/dL for men or 50 mg/dL for women.
  • Fasting blood glucose: 100 mg/dL or higher.
  • Blood pressure: the Cleveland Clinic lists systolic 130 mmHg or higher or diastolic 85 mmHg or higher, while Johns Hopkins lists 130/80 mm Hg or higher. Reference sources differ slightly, so ask your doctor which cutoff they use.

The Cleveland Clinic describes insulin resistance as the underlying driver. Cells respond less well to insulin, the pancreas produces more of it, and that contributes to weight gain around the middle, cardiovascular problems and fatty liver disease.

Why waist numbers differ in Korea

If you live in or are moving to Korea, the waist cutoff in local guidance is lower than the US figure. The Korean Society for the Study of Obesity guidelines define abdominal obesity as a waist of 90 cm or more for men and 85 cm or more for women. The same guidelines list the other four metabolic syndrome components as blood pressure of 130/85 mmHg or higher (or taking blood pressure medication), fasting glucose of 100 mg/dL or higher (or taking diabetes medication), triglycerides of 150 mg/dL or higher, and HDL below 40 mg/dL for men or 50 mg/dL for women.

The guidelines also set Korean BMI categories: 23.0 to 24.9 is classed as pre-obesity (overweight), 25.0 to 29.9 as class I obesity, and 30.0 to 34.9 as class II. These differ from the cutoffs many other countries use, which is another reason to read results against the standard your clinic applies.

Movement: the weekly targets

The World Health Organization recommends that adults do at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity per week, and says muscle strengthening benefits everyone. It also advises limiting sedentary time, noting that more sitting is linked to higher risk of cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes. WHO reports that 31 percent of adults globally do not reach the 150-minute threshold.

The Korean obesity guidelines give a similar message: at least 150 minutes per week of moderate aerobic activity, or three to five sessions a week, plus resistance training two to four times weekly using large muscle groups. A simple way to apply this is to split the 150 minutes into short walks across five days and add two strength sessions, then adjust to your own fitness and your doctor's advice.

Food: what the guidance actually says

WHO's healthy diet fact sheet gives specific numbers that are easy to check against your own day:

  • At least 400 grams of fruit and vegetables per day.
  • Free sugars under 10 percent of total energy intake, about 50 grams or 12 level teaspoons on a 2,000-calorie diet.
  • Total fat at 15 to 30 percent of energy, saturated fat under 10 percent and trans fat under 1 percent.
  • Salt under 5 grams per day, which is about 2 grams of sodium.
  • At least 25 grams of fiber daily for adults, with whole grains and legumes as carbohydrate sources.

The Cleveland Clinic and Johns Hopkins both point to a heart-healthy pattern built on whole grains, fruits, vegetables and healthy fats, and the Cleveland Clinic names a Mediterranean-style diet as recommended.

Weight, sleep and stress

You do not need dramatic weight loss to see a benefit. The Cleveland Clinic cites a study finding that losing 7 percent of excess weight reduced the onset of type 2 diabetes by 58 percent. The Korean guidelines set a goal of 5 to 10 percent body weight reduction within six months for people who need to lose weight, and they describe an energy restriction of 500 to 1,000 kilocalories a day as part of that approach, which is a decision to make with a clinician. The Cleveland Clinic also lists quality sleep, stress management and quitting smoking among core strategies. Johns Hopkins adds reducing alcohol.

A simple self-check routine

  1. Measure your waist at home with a tape and note the number.
  2. Book a health checkup that includes blood pressure, fasting glucose and a lipid panel (triglycerides and HDL).
  3. Compare your results with the five markers above, using the cutoffs your doctor applies.
  4. Pick one change in each area: a walking target, one swap in your diet, and a fixed bedtime.
  5. Repeat the tests after a few months so you can see whether the numbers moved.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Comparing your waist with the wrong cutoff. US and Korean figures are different.
  • Judging health by weight alone, when the five markers are what define the syndrome.
  • Relying on short-term crash diets. The sources above center on steady, repeatable habits.
  • Stopping prescribed medicine because numbers improved without asking your doctor first.
  • Treating one test result as a diagnosis. Fasting glucose, lipids and blood pressure should be interpreted by a clinician.

This is general information, not medical advice

Diagnostic thresholds and guidelines are updated by medical bodies, and your own risk depends on your age, family history and existing conditions. This article is general educational information and does not replace advice from a doctor. For testing and treatment, speak with a qualified clinician, and consult the Korean Society for the Study of Obesity or your national health authority for current guidelines.
