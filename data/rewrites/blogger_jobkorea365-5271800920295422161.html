If large conglomerates have stopped running a single big graduate intake, a job seeker in Korea has two practical moves: apply year-round to a wider range of employers, and use the government schemes that reward small and mid-sized companies for hiring young people. This article sets out what the sources I read say about both, and which details you should check before relying on them.

Why the old route has narrowed

A report summarising Statistics Korea's April economically active population survey says there were 228,000 "resting youth" aged 25 to 29, meaning people able to work who were neither employed nor looking, the highest April figure since 2020. The same report, citing the Korea Enterprises Federation's first-job transition survey, says large firms and conglomerates have moved away from mass graduate recruitment toward year-round and experienced-hire intakes. That is a secondary summary of the underlying surveys, so treat the numbers as indicative and check the original releases if you need them for a formal purpose.

The practical message is simple: a plan built around one autumn application window is fragile. Build a list of target employers across company sizes and check their postings regularly.

Government schemes that favour smaller employers

The Asia Business Daily describes the Youth Job Leap Incentive. For young workers at small and medium-sized enterprises in non-metropolitan areas, support is up to 7.2 million won over two years, up from a previous maximum of 4.8 million won, with the amount depending on the region category. The employee must stay employed for at least six months. Employers can receive up to 7.2 million won, 600,000 won a month over one year, subject to retaining the worker for six months or more. The article says metropolitan-area employees receive no separate continuous-service incentive.

The Seoul Economic Daily reports that the Ministry of Economy and Finance and the Ministry of Employment and Labor announced a plan to raise youth job spending from 4 trillion won to 5 trillion won next year. For young people hired by SMEs outside the capital region, it describes combined payments of about 1 million won a month for two years: a leap incentive of up to 750,000 won, government savings-account contributions of up to 200,000 won and a 10,000 won culture and arts pass. This is a budget plan, so the final scheme depends on approval and implementation.

The Youth New Deal tracks

Ajupress reports that the government announced a Youth New Deal implementation plan on April 29, 2026, aiming to support roughly 100,000 young people through three tracks:

  • Advancement: job training, including a K-New Deal Academy of 10,000 places in fields such as AI, semiconductors, finance and content, and a boot camp for 4,000 non-student job seekers.
  • Experience: 22,000 new work-experience slots in the public, social and private sectors, with records integrated through the Employment 24 platform.
  • Recovery: support services such as Youth Future Centers, expanded from 4 to 17 locations, and job-search allowances for 30,000 more people.

Priority goes to young people facing employment barriers, according to the same report. Whether you qualify depends on age, status and region, which differ by programme.

What employers must meet

The Asia Business Daily says that in the metropolitan area, priority-support enterprises with five or more employees can receive the employer incentive if they hire young people facing employment difficulties, defined as unemployed for four months or more or with a high school education or less. Outside the metropolitan area, priority-support enterprises or mid-sized companies in industrial complexes are covered. Ajupress reports that in the first quarter there were 171,000 unemployed young people and an employment rate of 43.5 percent for the group. When you talk to an employer, ask whether the company is eligible, because that can affect how it hires.

How to put this into a search plan

  1. List employers by size: a few large firms, but mostly mid-sized and small firms in the fields you can work in.
  2. Check each programme's eligibility rules on the official notice, including age, region, employer size and minimum employment period.
  3. Ask employers directly whether they take part in the youth incentive schemes. Employers who do may be more willing to hire first-time workers.
  4. Use training and work-experience programmes to fill gaps in your resume, then record them through official platforms where offered.
  5. Apply continuously rather than once, since year-round hiring is now common among larger firms too.

Common mistakes to verify

  • Counting on an incentive that is only a budget proposal.
  • Assuming the metropolitan area qualifies. The Leap incentive reports describe the larger benefits as applying outside the capital region.
  • Leaving a job before the six-month mark that the incentive requires.
  • Applying to an employer without checking whether it is a registered small or medium-sized enterprise, which the schemes require.
  • Reading a news summary as the rule. Use the Ministry of Employment and Labor notice for the exact conditions.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This article is general information and not career, legal or financial advice. Budgets, amounts and eligibility rules for youth employment schemes are revised each year. Confirm current conditions with the Ministry of Employment and Labor and the Employment 24 (Work24) service before you rely on any figure here.
