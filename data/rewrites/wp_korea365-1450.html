In Korea, bowing is a greeting that signals respect, and how deeply you bow generally follows the other person's age and rank. According to Cultural Atlas, the bow is the primary greeting and comes in casual, business and deep forms. Etiquette guides also tie it to a wider set of habits, including two-handed gestures, formal titles and the order of eating, all of which express the same idea of showing regard to someone older or senior. The sources used here are general cultural guides, so treat them as a starting point rather than a rulebook.

The three bows Cultural Atlas describes

Cultural Atlas separates bows by depth and purpose:

  • Casual bow: a dip of the head with the eyes closed, sometimes with a slight bend from the waist, suited to everyday greetings.
  • Business bow: the torso bends about 30 degrees.
  • Deep bow: the torso bends 45 degrees or more, used to express sincere gratitude or apology.

The same source says deeper bows are given to superiors and elders. A visitor does not need to master angles; a modest, sincere bow of the head and shoulders is the safe choice when unsure, and the 90 Day Korean etiquette guide advises that bowing is usually done to someone older or of higher rank and should be reciprocated when offered.

Bowing and handshakes together

Wikipedia's article on South Korean etiquette states that the bow is the traditional greeting, although it is often accompanied by a handshake among men. For a respectful handshake, the right forearm is supported by the left hand. Cultural Atlas explains that the right hand is preferred and adds that using the left hand alone can be viewed negatively, particularly by older Koreans or in formal settings, though most younger people will not take offence. It also says that supporting the right wrist with the left hand during a handshake shows respect.

The 90 Day Korean guide connects the two-handed idea to rank: it says one-handed gestures from a junior to a senior are considered disrespectful, and that two hands are used for handshakes, receiving items and pouring drinks.

How social ranking shows up beyond the bow

The sources describe several linked habits:

  • Titles: Cultural Atlas advises using formal titles such as Mr., Ms. or Doctor when first meeting someone, until they indicate otherwise.
  • Receiving things: Wikipedia says objects should be accepted with the right hand or with both hands, never the left hand alone, especially when taking a drink; the right hand holds the cup while the left supports the right wrist.
  • Business cards: the 90 Day Korean guide says to receive a card with both hands, look at it for several seconds and not write on it, since it represents the person.
  • Eye contact: Wikipedia says direct eye contact between juniors and seniors should be avoided because it can seem confrontational; this will vary with the setting and the individuals.
  • Dining: the 90 Day Korean guide says to wait for older people to begin eating, and Wikipedia says chopsticks and spoons should not be stood upright in food, particularly rice, because this resembles offerings at ancestral graves.
  • Language: the 90 Day Korean guide notes that polite speech endings are built into Korean grammar and advises learners to lean towards being more polite when unsure of the relationship.

Bowing at funerals is different

Wikipedia notes that bowing differs at funerals: one bow honours the living and two bows honour the deceased. It also says men and women place their hands differently, with men placing the left hand over the right and women the reverse. If you attend a funeral in Korea, watch what the family and other guests do and follow quietly, since customs can vary by family and religion.

A practical guide for visitors and new colleagues

  1. Return a bow. If someone bows to you, bow back.
  2. Bow lower to elders and seniors. A deeper bow suits greater age or rank, according to Cultural Atlas.
  3. Use the right hand, or both hands, to give and receive. Avoid using the left alone for formal exchanges.
  4. Offer a handshake only after observing, and support the right forearm if you do.
  5. Use titles at first meeting and follow the other person's lead on informality.
  6. Take your cue from the host at a meal.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Ignoring depth. Cultural Atlas ties the depth of a bow to rank and purpose, so a deep bow suits gratitude or apology and a lighter one suits everyday greetings.
  • Using one hand for a senior. This is the gesture the guides flag as disrespectful.
  • Treating a guide as universal. Cultural Atlas and Wikipedia describe general norms; younger people, international workplaces and individual preferences differ.
  • Copying funeral customs without guidance. Follow the family.
  • Staring in formal situations. Some guides say prolonged eye contact with seniors can seem confrontational.

Customs vary: confirm in context

This article is general cultural information, not a rulebook, and manners differ by region, generation, workplace and family. The sources are a cultural reference site, a community-edited encyclopedia and a language-learning guide, not official bodies, so for formal occasions, business protocol or ceremonies, ask your host, employer or a local contact what is expected.
