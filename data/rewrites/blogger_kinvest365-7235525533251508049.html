The KOSPI is currently driven by two companies. The Seoul Economic Daily reports that Samsung Electronics and SK hynix together make up 50.85% of KOSPI market capitalization, and The Korea Herald reports that foreign investors sold a net 20.305 trillion won of Korean stocks between September 1 and October 2, led by those same two names. That makes concentration, foreign flows and valuation the three numbers worth tracking. This article sets out what the sources say about each and what they do not.

Concentration: two stocks, half the index

Seoul Economic Daily's figure of 50.85% is the clearest measure of how much the index depends on semiconductors. AMRO, the ASEAN+3 Macroeconomic Research Office, adds that Samsung Electronics and SK hynix together accounted for about half of the increase in total KOSPI market capitalization over the previous year. When two stocks carry this much weight, a headline index move can mostly reflect those two.

The trade data point the same way. The Korea Times, citing the industry ministry, reports that September exports reached USD 120.9 billion, up 83.5% from a year earlier, with semiconductor exports at USD 60.3 billion, up 263%. Automobile exports, by contrast, were USD 6.05 billion, down 5.5%. Strength in chips is not the same as strength across the economy.

Volatility: what the foreign selling looked like

According to The Korea Herald, foreign investors were net buyers on only 7 of 22 trading days between September 1 and October 2, and the largest single-day outflow, on September 28, exceeded 3 trillion won. It names SK hynix (12.009 trillion won of net sales) and Samsung Electronics (5.186 trillion won) as the most affected. Foreign ownership of SK hynix fell to 49.76%, its lowest level in more than two years, while ownership of Samsung Electronics was 46.41% and of the KOSPI overall 39.66%.

Seoul Economic Daily describes September as the fifth straight month of net foreign selling, with 134.92 trillion won sold over five months and 21.51 trillion won in September alone. It cites profit-taking after the earlier chip rally, tensions involving the United States and Iran, and rising global rates; it notes the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield at 5.29%. The KOSPI closed at 6,838.04 in one session and 7,003.74 in another reported around the start of October, so daily moves of several hundred points between sessions are plausible in this market.

Valuation: cheap on average, uneven underneath

AMRO's report gives the most useful valuation context. It puts Korea's 10-year average price-to-book ratio at 0.99, against 2.65 for advanced economies and 1.61 for emerging markets, and says Korea's ratio had risen to 1.35 by the previous year-end. It also says around 70 percent of individual stocks still trade below a price-to-book ratio of 1, and that more than 60 percent of firms have a return on equity below Korea's long-term average of 7 percent.

Payouts matter as well. AMRO reports a 10-year average dividend payout ratio of 31.3% in Korea, compared with a 40% to 60% range in advanced economies and peers. The sources used here do not give a current price-to-earnings ratio for the index, so check that with the exchange or your broker rather than relying on an old figure.

Reforms and index status

AMRO says that by the previous year-end 171 firms, about 6 percent of listed companies and 44.5% of market capitalization, had joined the Value-up program. Large companies were 64% of participants. Separately, The Korea Times reports that on June 24 MSCI kept Korea off its Developed Market watchlist, citing limited offshore won convertibility, restricted use of omnibus accounts and in-kind transfers, and operational challenges after the short-selling ban. MSCI wanted evidence that reforms last, including mandatory English disclosures and a 24-hour foreign exchange market planned to start in July.

A simple way to read the numbers

  1. Check how much of any index move came from Samsung Electronics and SK hynix alone.
  2. Look at foreign flows over weeks, not one day.
  3. Compare a company's price-to-book ratio and return on equity with its own history and sector, not only with the market average.
  4. Look at payout ratios and any Value-up disclosure the company has filed.
  5. Note the date on every figure you use.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Using the index as a proxy for the average Korean company. The index is dominated by two stocks.
  • Calling the market cheap from one ratio. AMRO's average hides a wide spread across companies.
  • Mixing dates. Foreign ownership and index levels change daily.
  • Forgetting currency. Returns for a foreign investor include the won exchange rate.
