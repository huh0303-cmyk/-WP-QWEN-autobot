A 30-minute daily TOPIK routine works best when each block has a single job: retrieve vocabulary from memory, listen to something short, read one passage, and spend the last minutes checking mistakes. Vocabulary review should be spaced over days, and you should test yourself rather than only re-read. Use the exam format to decide how to rotate the blocks, and add a longer weekly session for full sections.

Start from what the exam asks you to do

According to the Wikipedia overview of the Test of Proficiency in Korean, TOPIK has two tiers. TOPIK I (levels 1 and 2) has a listening section of 40 minutes and 30 questions and a reading section of 60 minutes and 40 questions. TOPIK II (levels 3 to 6) has listening (60 minutes, 50 questions), writing (50 minutes, 4 questions) and reading (70 minutes, 50 questions). The TOPIK Guide site gives the same question counts and adds that TOPIK II writing has two short-answer questions and two essay questions, with essays of roughly 200 to 300 characters and 600 to 700 characters.

Both sources say the score is valid for two years. TOPIK Guide notes it can be used for admission to Korean universities and for job-seeking at Korean companies and public institutions. Check your target university's own requirement, since schools set their own level cut-offs.

The 30-minute structure

  1. 10 minutes - vocabulary. Review a small set of words, cover the meaning, and try to recall it before checking. Write one example sentence for each word you missed.
  2. 10 minutes - listening. Use one short Korean clip. Listen once for the main idea and once more for details.
  3. 5 minutes - reading. Read one short paragraph and state its main point before looking up unknown words.
  4. 5 minutes - review. Write down what you got wrong and the one thing to fix tomorrow.

Keep the sets small enough to finish. A routine you complete on a busy day is worth more than an ambitious one you skip.

Why the vocabulary block uses recall and spacing

Wikipedia's article on spaced repetition describes it as a flashcard-based method in which new and harder cards appear more often and older, easier ones less often, exploiting the spacing effect. It says the approach is particularly effective for second-language vocabulary, where learners must retain many items, and traces the forgetting curve to Hermann Ebbinghaus in the 1880s. It also mentions the Leitner system, which uses five compartments with longer review intervals.

The article on the testing effect says long-term memory improves when part of the learning period is spent retrieving information from memory, and that short-answer testing is described as more effective than multiple choice. In practice that means covering the answer and producing the Korean word or meaning yourself, instead of only re-reading a list. The same article warns that some studies show mixed results in specific contexts and that much research comes from a narrow set of countries, so treat it as a strong habit, not a guarantee.

Rotate the blocks to match your level

  • Preparing for TOPIK I: reading carries more minutes and questions than listening in the exam, so shift 5 minutes from listening to reading on some days.
  • Preparing for TOPIK II: add short writing practice. Write two sentences from your vocabulary set on weekdays and one longer paragraph on the weekend.
  • Short on time: keep the vocabulary and review blocks, since they take only 15 minutes and protect your memory of what you already learned.

Add a weekly full-length check

Thirty minutes a day does not rehearse test stamina. Once a week, time one full section under exam conditions, using the section lengths above. Mark errors by type: vocabulary you did not know, grammar you misread, or listening you missed in the first pass. Feed the vocabulary errors into next week's flashcards.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Re-reading word lists instead of recalling from memory.
  • Reviewing only new words and never the old ones.
  • Using clips that are too long, so you stop listening for details.
  • Ignoring the writing section until the last month if you are aiming for TOPIK II.
  • Assuming a certain level is enough for your university without checking its own requirement.
  • Booking a test date without checking the official schedule. The sources do not agree on the months, and tests in Korea and overseas can differ.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

TOPIK format, fees, test dates and registration are set by the exam organizers and can change, and university or visa language requirements are set by each institution or authority. Confirm current details on the official TOPIK website and with the admissions office of your target school. This is general study information, not an assurance of a score.
