Korean universities run a special admission route for Korean students who studied abroad, and it comes in two main forms: a "3-year" route for students who studied abroad for part of secondary school while a parent worked overseas, and a "12-year" route for students who completed the whole school curriculum abroad. Which one you can use depends on where and how long you studied, and each university sets its own selection method and dates. The only reliable answer for your case is the current admission guide of the specific university.

The two routes in plain terms

According to a description of the system on NamuWiki, a user-edited encyclopedia, the "3-year" screening requires at least three grades of middle school and one year of high school abroad, and it is limited to 2% of total admissions and under 10% per recruitment unit. The "12-year" screening requires completing all of elementary, middle and high school overseas, and at most universities there is no cap on the number admitted.

A 2027 admission guide from Kumoh National Institute of Technology, a university document, describes the same split. Its first pathway covers children of overseas workers, with admission within 2% of total enrolment, and its second pathway covers completion of all 12 years of primary, middle and secondary education abroad, with no quota limit. These are the rules of one university, so use them as an example of the structure rather than a universal rule.

What the 3-year route asks for

Kumoh's guide lists these conditions for the children-of-overseas-workers pathway:

  • A parent employed abroad for at least three years, stated as 1,095 days.
  • The student completing at least three years of middle and high school, including at least one year of high school, in the country where the parent worked.
  • Time spent abroad each year: the student three quarters of the year and the parents two thirds of the year.
  • High school graduation.

A private admissions guide, Eduify, makes the same point in stricter terms: residence is counted by the school calendar rather than the calendar year, and vacation stays in Korea count against the overseas-presence thresholds. Its description of the parent condition differs in wording from Kumoh's guide, which is a reason to read your target university's own text. The Eduify guide also says that parental study abroad, as opposed to employment, does not qualify for the 3-year route, and that individual universities decide eligibility within government guidelines.

What the 12-year route asks for

Kumoh describes this pathway as completion of all 12 years of primary, middle and secondary education equivalent to the Korean system, with a foreign high school diploma. NamuWiki says there is no requirement about the parents' work or the student's residence in this category, and Eduify says the same, adding that the documented coursework must total 23 semesters, allowing one semester to be lost to differences between school systems. That figure comes from a private guide, so confirm it with the university.

In practice, a student who moved schools, repeated a grade or spent a period in Korean schools should have each school record checked against the university's wording before applying.

Documents and selection methods differ by university

Kumoh lists a high school diploma with translation, attendance records, transcripts, entry and exit records and passport copies. It asks for an overseas Koreans registration certificate unless the applicant provides residence proof such as a visa, a property contract, insurance documents or tax receipts. Foreign-language documents need translation. Eduify's list for the 3-year route adds entry-exit certificates for the student and parents, parent employment and tax records, and apostilled or consulate-certified school documents.

Selection methods vary. Kumoh converts high school grades to letter grades and then to a 100-to-0 point scale, averaged, with tie-breakers of mathematics scores and then longer overseas study. NamuWiki says other universities use document screening (it names Seoul National, Yonsei and Korea University), written exams (Konkuk, Kyung Hee, Inha, Dankook, Ajou), interviews (Kwangwoon, Myongji, Sangmyung) or a mix. Because that comes from a wiki and may be out of date, check each school's current guide.

How the rules changed over time

NamuWiki says the system was established in 1978, originally for children of public officials and researchers working abroad, and later expanded to all Koreans with overseas work experience. It adds that older variants with 2-year, 6-year and 9-year requirements were abolished by 2020, when parental qualifications and student residence conditions were standardised across universities. Treat this history as background from a wiki, not an official record.

Steps to check your own eligibility

  1. Write a timeline of every school you attended, with country, dates and grade levels.
  2. For the 3-year route, add each parent's overseas work period and the student's days abroad per school year.
  3. Open the current guide of each university you are considering and copy its eligibility wording next to your timeline.
  4. Note the application period and the language of the application. Kumoh's guide says Korean is required for the application and English translations are mandatory for foreign documents.
  5. Ask the admissions office to confirm your case in writing before you pay for apostilles or translations.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Counting calendar years, not school years. Residence is tracked against the academic calendar in the guides above.
  • Forgetting short stays in Korea. Vacation time in Korea can reduce your overseas days.
  • Treating one university's rules as universal. Dates, quotas and methods differ.
  • Relying on a blog or wiki. Confirm with the university's own admission guide.
  • Missing documents. Entry and exit records, and proof of employment, can take weeks to obtain.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This article is general information, not legal or immigration advice, and it does not decide whether you qualify. Admission rules, quotas and dates are published by each university every year, and the details can change. Confirm everything with the admissions office of the university you are applying to and, for questions about residency status or visas, with HiKorea or the Korean embassy or consulate in your country.
