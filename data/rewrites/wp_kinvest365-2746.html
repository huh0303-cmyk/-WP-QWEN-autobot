The National Pension Service (NPS) manages the National Pension Fund, which its own fund site describes as the world's third-largest pension fund by total assets, valued at KRW 1,684.2 trillion at the end of July 2026. For an investor, the practical questions are who sets its asset mix, what that mix looks like now, and how its plans could affect Korean stocks and the won. These seven questions are based on the NPS fund site and recent Korea Herald and Seoul Economic Daily reporting.

1. How large is the fund, and what has it earned?

According to the NPS fund overview, the fund stood at KRW 1,684.2 trillion at end-July 2026. The same page reports a preliminary year-to-date return of 14.84% for January to July 2026, a three-year return of 16.05% (covering 2023 to 2025) and a return since inception in 1988 of 8.04% (to 2025). The Korea Herald reported that the fund began 2025 at KRW 1,212 trillion, so the size has grown quickly. These are the fund's own reported figures; past returns do not predict future ones.

2. How is the money split across asset classes?

The NPS table for end-July 2026 shows the following:

Compare this with the end-2024 mix cited from NPS data by Top1000funds: global equity 35.54%, domestic fixed income 28.39%, alternatives 17.06%, domestic equity 11.52% and global fixed income 7.28%. Domestic equity's share more than doubled in between. Part of a share change can come from market price movements rather than from buying or selling, so the table shows a position on a date, not a decision.

3. Who decides the targets?

The Korea Herald reported that the NPS fund management committee decided the 2026 targets on January 26, 2026, with the Health and Welfare Minister commenting on the decision. The Seoul Economic Daily reported that the National Pension Fund Management Committee held its fourth meeting on May 15, 2026 to review a mid-term asset allocation plan for 2027 to 2031. According to that report, the committee deliberates and resolves target weightings by asset class for the following five years by the end of May each year. For investors, the committee's decisions, not NPS fund managers alone, set the direction.

4. What are the current targets?

The Korea Herald reported the 2026 targets as: domestic equities raised from 14.4% to 14.9%, overseas equities lowered from 38.9% to 37.2%, and domestic bonds raised from 23.7% to 24.9%. The committee cited shifts in market conditions at home and overseas, concerns about foreign exchange market conditions and the rising burden of securing foreign currency as the fund's assets keep expanding. Note that actual weights, such as the 24.9% domestic equity share in the table above, can sit well away from the target figures.

5. What longer-term direction has been reported?

An earlier Korea Herald article described plans to lift the global share of assets to 60% by 2028 and the overall stock allocation to 55% by 2029, together with a new reference portfolio framework that divides assets into risk and non-risk categories with a long-term goal of 65% risk assets. A later Korea Herald article reported stockholdings above 50% for the first time, at 51.6% at the end of August 2025. Because the targets were revised in January 2026 and a new five-year plan was being deliberated in May 2026, treat the older figures as history and check the latest committee decision.

6. Why does the won matter to the fund's plans?

The January 2026 committee decision explicitly referred to foreign exchange market conditions and the burden of securing foreign currency. Holding overseas assets requires foreign currency, and the committee named that as a consideration. The reports do not give a mechanical rule linking NPS decisions to the exchange rate, so an investor should not infer one.

7. What should an outside investor actually track?

  1. The fund site's monthly allocation table for the actual mix.
  2. The committee's annual allocation decisions, normally resolved by the end of May for the five-year plan period.
  3. Revisions to short-term targets, such as the January change.
  4. The stated reasons, particularly market conditions and foreign exchange.
  5. Reporting dates: the site gives preliminary figures that can be revised.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Confusing targets with holdings. A target is a policy weight; the table shows the actual weight on a date.
  • Quoting older figures as current. Several widely repeated numbers belong to 2024 or 2025 reporting.
  • Treating news summaries as official. Check the fund site or the Ministry of Health and Welfare release for the final numbers.
  • Reading NPS positioning as a recommendation. A pension fund's allocation reflects its own liabilities and mandate, not yours.
