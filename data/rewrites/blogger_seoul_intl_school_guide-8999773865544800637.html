Yes, you can build Korean from zero without paying for classes, and still prepare for the TOPIK exam that most Korean universities ask about. The Online King Sejong Institute is a free online Korean learning service run by the King Sejong Institute Foundation, and its lesson levels run from Hangeul basics to advanced Korean. This article explains what that platform offers, how its courses are organised, and how to plan your study around the way TOPIK is built.

What the Online King Sejong Institute offers

According to the Online King Sejong Institute's own "about" page, the service is described as a free online Korean language learning service that works on PC and mobile devices. It is aimed at any foreign learner who wants to learn Korean, and it covers levels "from introductory to advanced."

The same page describes two forms of the service: a general Online King Sejong Institute open to any learner, and a Collaboration version for students of institutions that have an agreement with the foundation. If you study at a school that mentions such an agreement, ask your teacher which version you should register for.

Beyond language lessons, the platform lists K-Culture courses. These are separate from the level-based Korean curriculum, so treat them as extra reading and listening material rather than a replacement for the main lessons.

How the curriculum is organised

The curriculum page lists six proficiency steps: Introductory, Beginner 1 and 2, Intermediate 1 and 2, and Advanced 1 and 2. The lesson counts it gives are:

  • Introductory: 12 lessons, focused on Hangeul and basic vocabulary.
  • Beginner, Intermediate and Advanced: 40 lessons each, split into two parts of 20 lessons.
  • Business Korean: 24 lessons in two parts of 12.
  • Reading and Writing: 30 lessons, 15 for reading and 15 for writing.

The page also says that introductory and beginner courses are available in 15 languages, including Vietnamese, while intermediate and advanced courses are taught in Korean only. If your first language is on that list, you can start with explanations you understand. Once you reach the intermediate steps, expect all instructions to be in Korean.

Three course types: pick the one you will finish

The platform offers three delivery formats: Lecture Assisted, Learning Assisted and Self-learning. The curriculum page describes the first as instructor-led and the second as a blended approach with instructor support, while the third is independent study.

The right choice depends on your habits, not on ambition. If you tend to drop self-study after two weeks, a type with instructor involvement may keep you on schedule. If your timetable changes from week to week, self-learning gives the most flexibility. Check each course listing on the platform for its schedule and any enrolment conditions before you commit.

How this study connects to TOPIK

The National Institute for International Education (NIIED) describes TOPIK as a test for non-native speakers, including people seeking university admission in Korea and job applicants. It has two main levels. TOPIK I covers levels 1 and 2 and consists of Listening (30 items) and Reading (40 items). TOPIK II covers levels 3 to 6 and consists of Listening (50 items), Reading (50 items) and Writing (4 tasks). NIIED also lists a separate Speaking test with six tasks over 30 minutes.

Study in Korea, the government portal for international students, states that students generally need TOPIK level 3 or higher to be admitted and level 4 or higher to graduate, and that exchange students, scholarship recipients and some other groups have different requirements. The portal also names the Nuri King Sejong Institute among online options for learning Korean.

One thing the sources do not say is that a given Sejong level equals a given TOPIK level. Neither the Sejong curriculum page nor the NIIED page publishes such a conversion, so do not assume that finishing Intermediate 1 means you will score level 3. Use the lessons to build skills, then check your real position with a practice test or a TOPIK past paper.

A weekly routine built around the exam sections

Because TOPIK tests listening and reading in every version, and writing only in TOPIK II, you can divide your week by skill. A simple pattern:

  1. Spend most sessions on the platform's lessons, since vocabulary and grammar feed every section.
  2. Add a short listening block every day, even ten minutes, because listening is tested in both TOPIK I and II.
  3. Read short texts aloud and silently, and note unknown words, since reading carries 40 items in TOPIK I and 50 in TOPIK II.
  4. Once you move into the intermediate steps, use the Reading and Writing lessons to practise short written answers by hand, because TOPIK II has four writing tasks.
  5. Every few weeks, take a timed practice section under exam conditions.

This routine is a study suggestion, not an official programme. The exam format itself, including the number of items and the sections, is what the NIIED page confirms.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Assuming "free" means certified. The platform is free to use, but a completed lesson is not a TOPIK score. Universities that ask for TOPIK want the exam result.
  • Skipping Hangeul. The introductory course exists for a reason; unstable reading of Hangeul slows every later step.
  • Studying only one skill. Strong vocabulary with no listening practice leaves a gap on exam day.
  • Ignoring each university's own rule. Study in Korea says requirements vary by type of university and programme, so read the admission guide of each school you apply to.
  • Relying on an old test date. Check the current schedule on the official TOPIK pages before you plan your calendar.

Rules change: confirm with the official bodies

Course lists, languages offered and exam arrangements can change. This article is general information about learning Korean, not admission or visa advice. Confirm course details on the Online King Sejong Institute site, TOPIK details with NIIED, and admission requirements with each university you plan to apply to.
