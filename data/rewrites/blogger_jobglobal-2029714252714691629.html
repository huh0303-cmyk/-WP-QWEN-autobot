An inclusive job description in Korea is one that lists only requirements the job actually needs, says honestly what the employer can and cannot offer, and avoids the personal details that Korean hiring law restricts. The checks below apply to ads for foreign and Korean candidates alike, and they also shape how interviewers should be briefed.

What Korean law says about requirements in an ad

Three statutes matter most for the wording of a posting, based on the English translations published by the Korea Legislation Research Institute (KLRI).

  • Fair Hiring Procedure Act. A job offerer may not place a false hiring advertisement, for instance to collect ideas or promote a business site, and may not change the ad or working conditions to the applicant's disadvantage without justifiable grounds (Article 4).
  • Equal Employment Opportunity and Work-Family Balance Assistance Act. An employer may not demand physical conditions, including appearance, height or weight, or unmarried status, that are not required for performing the duties (Article 7).
  • Framework Act on Employment Policy. In recruiting, business owners must not discriminate without a rational reason on grounds including gender, religion, age, social status, place of origin, alma mater, marriage, pregnancy and medical history (Article 19).

The Korea Herald adds that Article 4-3 of the Fair Hiring Procedure Act bars requests for information unnecessary to the job, such as height, weight, place of birth, marital status, family members' employment and property.

Separate must-have skills from nice-to-have preferences

A posting is easier to defend, and easier for candidates to read, when each line answers the question "does the person need this to do the work?" A practical way to check:

  1. List the duties first, in plain verbs, such as "prepare monthly invoices" or "answer customer emails in English and Korean".
  2. Turn each duty into a requirement: a skill, a certificate or a level of language.
  3. Mark anything left over as a preference, or delete it.

Language is a good example. If the role involves customer calls in Korean, say so and name the level you need. If the work is done in English, say that instead. Vague phrases such as "native-level" or "good communication" push out capable applicants without telling anyone what you need.

Be clear about visa and sponsorship facts

Foreign applicants want to know early whether the employer can support a visa. The Korea Immigration Service's Visa Navigator explains that professional employment statuses E-1 to E-7 cover work within the occupational scope of each status, and that a D-10 job-seeker visa is limited to specific activities such as internships and, for certain graduates, part-time work. A posting can state which statuses the employer can accept and whether it will assist with a change of status. It should not promise an outcome, because the immigration office decides.

Employers who recruit international graduates can also reach them through the K-Work platform. According to Korea.net, the platform was launched by the Ministry of SMEs and Startups and the Korea SMEs and Startups Agency, and it offers AI-based job recommendations and support for converting a D-2 or D-10 visa to an E-7-1 work visa.

Check the full set of ad channels

The Korea Herald reported that the Labor Ministry planned upgrades to its Worknet platform, including legal pop-ups and automatic filtering of hiring postings that contain inappropriate keywords. If you post on public job boards, expect that wording such as requirements on looks or marital status may be flagged. Review each ad against the three statutes above before it goes live.

Prepare interviewers, not only the ad

A fair job description can be undone in the interview room. KoWork, a job site for foreigners, notes that personal and cultural questions can come up in Korean interviews, particularly at smaller companies. Brief your panel on what they should not ask. Yale's Office of Career Strategy describes behavioral interviews, which ask candidates to describe something they did in the past, and recommends structuring answers as situation, task, action and result. Asking every candidate the same job-related past-experience questions, and scoring them against the same criteria, keeps the conversation on the work.

Also remember the process steps after the interview. Under the Fair Hiring Procedure Act, applicants are to be told without delay whether they are hired (Article 10), and may claim their documents back (Article 11).

A pre-publication checklist

  1. Read each requirement and ask whether the job cannot be done without it.
  2. Remove any wording about appearance, height, weight, marital status, family background or property.
  3. State the language level and the language of daily work.
  4. Say whether the employer can support a visa application, without promising the result.
  5. Describe pay, hours and place of work as they will actually be, so the offer later matches the ad.
  6. Have a second person, ideally someone from HR or a labor professional, read the ad before it is posted.

Common mistakes to verify

  • Adding age, appearance or marital-status preferences "just as a preference".
  • Copying a foreign-market template that lists benefits or conditions the company does not offer, which risks a false or misleading advertisement.
  • Leaving out the language level, so applicants guess.
  • Promising visa sponsorship without checking whether the role and the candidate fit an eligible status.
  • Posting the same ad on several boards and updating only one of them.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This article is general information and not legal advice. Hiring and employment laws are amended from time to time, and the KLRI translations may lag behind the Korean text. Confirm current requirements with the Ministry of Employment and Labor, and ask a qualified labor attorney or certified labor consultant (노무사) to review job advertisement wording for your company.
