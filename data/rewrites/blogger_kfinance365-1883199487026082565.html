With the Bank of Korea's base rate now at 3.00 percent, a household with a variable-rate loan should work out how much each further rate rise would add to its payments, and compare that with the extra cost of switching to a fixed rate. Data from the Bank of Korea and news reports show that fixed-rate borrowing has been shrinking as a share of loans, which means many households are exposed to rate rises.

Where rates and household debt stand

The Bank of Korea's Monetary Policy Board raised the base rate by 25 basis points to 3.00 percent from 2.75 percent on August 27, 2026. Its statement said housing prices in Seoul and the surrounding area continued to rise at a high pace while household loans also increased substantially, and that it was necessary to keep paying attention to both. The Korea Times reports that total household credit reached 2,019.8 trillion won in the second quarter, the first time it passed 2,000 trillion won since records began in 2002, with a quarterly rise of 25.9 trillion won that was the largest since the third quarter of 2021.

The Korea Times also reports that self-employed loans reached 1,095 trillion won in the first quarter, and that about 1.6 million self-employed people carry three or more loans, with combined debt of 645 trillion won. Whether you are an employee or run a business, the point is the same: the more separate loans you hold, the more reset dates you have to track.

What the rate rise can mean in won

The Korea Times estimates that the 0.25 percentage point increase will add about 1.8 trillion won a year to self-employed borrowers' interest costs, around 560,000 won per borrower, on the assumption that 65.5 percent of their loans carry variable rates.

You can run the same arithmetic for your own loan. As an illustration only, a variable-rate balance of 100 million won would cost 250,000 won more in interest over a year after a 0.25 percentage point rise, if nothing else changes (100,000,000 x 0.0025). Check your loan agreement for how often and by what benchmark your rate resets.

Fixed or variable: what the data shows

The Seoul Economic Daily, citing Bank of Korea data, reports that in July fixed-rate mortgages made up 31.9 percent of new home loans, the lowest in 12 years and five months, and fixed-rate loans were 21.0 percent of all household loans. The fixed mortgage rate was 4.76 percent against 4.35 percent for variable, a gap of 0.41 points. A Bank of Korea official was quoted as saying that because fixed rates are higher than variable rates right now, consumers appear to be choosing the cheaper rate for the moment.

A later Seoul Economic Daily report on August data says the fixed share of mortgages rose to 35.3 percent from 31.9 percent, and for household loans overall to 23.5 percent from 21 percent. The fixed mortgage rate rose 0.12 points to 4.88 percent, the 11th consecutive monthly rise. The article says the shift reflects borrowers responding to faster rate increases.

A simple way to decide

  1. Find your rate type and reset date in the loan contract: variable, fixed, or something in between.
  2. Calculate the monthly change for a rise of 0.25 and 0.50 percentage points using your outstanding balance.
  3. Compare with your slack: after essential expenses, could you absorb that extra amount every month?
  4. Ask your lender in writing what the fixed-rate offer would be, whether any fee applies to moving from your current loan, and what the new payment schedule would be.
  5. Decide on cash-flow, not prediction. The Bank of Korea board's July statement said it would continue a policy stance consistent with further rate hikes, but no source can tell you where rates will go.

Other steps that reduce interest exposure

  • Make extra principal repayments where your loan allows it. Ask the lender whether any early repayment charge applies before you do.
  • If you hold several loans, list them by interest rate and rate type so you can see which carry the most risk.
  • Keep a cash reserve so a payment rise does not force you into new borrowing.
  • If you are struggling, talk to your lender early. The Korea Times says the government plans debt relief measures for pandemic-era business loans, so ask whether any scheme applies to you.

Common mistakes to verify

  • Comparing only the starting rate. A lower variable rate can end up costing more if rates keep rising.
  • Assuming the fixed-share statistics describe all borrowers. They are averages for new loans or for the household loan stock as reported.
  • Switching without asking about fees or about how long the fixed period lasts.
  • Ignoring other loans, such as credit lines or business loans in your own name.
  • Using a headline rate rather than the rate in your contract.
