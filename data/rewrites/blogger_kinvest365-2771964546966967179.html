Korea's industrial land is divided into four types of complex, and a foreign company usually reaches it through a lease inside a Foreign Investment Zone. According to the KOTRA guide Industrial Sites in Korea, the four types are national, general, urban high-tech, and agricultural and industrial complexes. This article explains how they differ, how foreign investors get land, and what to check before choosing a site.

The four types of industrial complex

KOTRA's guide describes them this way:

  • National industrial complexes are designated by the national government to promote key industries and regional development.
  • General industrial complexes are designated by local governments to support regional revitalization.
  • Urban high-tech industrial complexes are built for knowledge-based and high-technology businesses in urban areas.
  • Agricultural and industrial complexes aim to raise rural income for farmers and fishermen.

The guide's mid-year count was 1,315 designated complexes: 51 national, 735 general, 46 urban high-tech and 483 agricultural. Sales rates were 97.3% for national complexes and 72.2% for urban high-tech ones. A high sales rate suggests that vacant plots in national complexes may be scarce, so ask early what land is actually available. Counts change as new complexes are designated, so use the current KOTRA or local government figure.

How foreign companies obtain land

The KOTRA guide lists three routes: long-term leases of up to 50 years in foreign investment zones, purchase after meeting occupancy requirements and investment thresholds, and a lease-to-own route with an initial five-year mandatory lease. It gives a typical annual lease of 1% of acquisition cost, with possible reductions linked to investment amount and job creation. These are general terms, and individual complexes set their own conditions.

Invest KOREA's page on Foreign Investment Zones describes three zone types. Complex-type zones sit inside existing industrial complexes, aim at small and medium foreign investors, and offer long-term low-cost leases rather than ownership, with a minimum 30% foreign share and a minimum investment of KRW 100 million. Individual-type zones are for large investors who pick their own site, with minimums of USD 30 million for manufacturing, USD 20 million for tourism and USD 10 million for logistics. Service-type zones are for high value-added services such as research and development. The page's zone counts date from an earlier year, so treat them only as an indication of the mix.

Which zone type fits which investor

Invest KOREA's zone page separates the options by size. A small or medium foreign investor with a minimum 30% foreign share and at least KRW 100 million can look at a complex-type zone inside an existing industrial complex and lease land instead of buying it. A large manufacturer investing at least USD 30 million, or a tourism or logistics investor above the lower thresholds, can look at an individual-type zone where it chooses its own site. A research or high value-added service company would look at the service-type zone, where the page says the minimum investment must equal the land or building lease price, the business must employ five to 15 full-time workers depending on the industry, and the investment must be completed within three years of the occupancy contract.

The point is that the zone type is determined partly by the size of the investment, so decide your budget and share structure before you shortlist locations.

Incentives that can apply

On the same page, Invest KOREA lists local tax exemption of up to 15 years and a five-year exemption from tariffs and VAT on capital goods imports. It also lists rent reductions for complex-type zones: 75% for manufacturing investments of USD 5 million or more, and 100% for 10 years for new growth engine technologies of USD 1 million or more. Investors must complete their investment within five years of signing the occupancy contract in complex-type zones.

Invest KOREA's cash grant page adds that grants require a foreign investment ratio of 30% or more and cover projects such as factory construction for new growth engine technologies, high-tech manufacturing, selected materials, parts and equipment production, and R&D facilities. It also lists a minimum of five full-time researchers and job creation above industry benchmarks, and says investments financed with long-term loans are excluded.

How to choose a complex

  1. Start with your industry. Check which complexes designate it as a permitted or target use.
  2. Ask the managing body how much land is vacant and when it can be occupied.
  3. Compare lease and purchase terms for your investment size against the zone type thresholds above.
  4. List incentive conditions: foreign share, minimum investment, hiring numbers, completion deadline.
  5. Check transport links, suppliers and labor supply for your own operation.
  6. Get every incentive and price assumption confirmed in writing.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Assuming any complex accepts any business. Each has designated industries.
  • Missing the deadline. Complex-type zones allow five years from the occupancy contract.
  • Financing with loans and expecting a grant. Invest KOREA excludes loan-financed investments.
  • Using national averages for a local deal. Lease reductions and local taxes follow local rules.

Rules change: confirm with the official bodies

This is general information, not legal, tax or investment advice. Zone rules, incentives and land availability change. Confirm current terms with KOTRA's Invest KOREA, the managing body of the complex and the local government, and obtain advice from a qualified professional before you commit.
