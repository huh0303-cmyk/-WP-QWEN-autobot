Your first weeks in Korea as a degree student come down to four jobs: register as a foreign resident within 90 days of entry, settle your housing and address, open a bank account and phone plan, and budget for monthly costs. According to the Study in Korea portal, students staying more than 90 days must complete foreigner registration within 90 days of arrival. This article follows that order and uses the portal's figures so that you can plan with real numbers.

Foreigner registration comes first

Study in Korea, run by the National Institute for International Education, states that foreigners who intend to stay more than 90 days must register within 90 days of entry. For students, it lists these documents: an application form, your passport, one passport-size photo taken within the past six months, a certificate of enrolment issued after entry, and the fee. A tuberculosis examination confirmation applies only to certain people, mainly visa holders from before a 2016 rule change.

The portal also asks for documents verifying your place of residence, so a signed dormitory allocation or a lease matters early. That is one reason a dormitory place can simplify your first weeks: your address and your registration documents come from the same place. Ask your university's international office how registration is handled, because many universities guide new students through it.

Two later obligations are worth noting now. A registered foreigner who moves must report the new address within 15 days, at the immigration office or the local township or municipal office. The portal says failing to report on time breaks Article 36 of the Immigration Act and may result in a fine of up to 1 million won.

Housing: dormitory, homestay or private rental

The portal describes three common options. University dormitories are usually on or near campus and relatively affordable, with libraries, computer labs and dining halls, but privacy is limited and the rules are strict. Rooms are single, double or quadruple occupancy, and conditions and costs vary by university, so ask the dormitory office. Homestays provide a room and meals for a monthly fee, and the portal advises visiting in person before deciding.

For private rentals, the portal explains two terms. In a jeonse lease, the tenant pays a large lump sum and the deposit is returned at the end of the term, with no monthly rent. In a wolse lease, the tenant pays a deposit plus a fixed monthly rent, and the deposit is returned when the lease ends. The portal says many international students rent privately on a monthly basis, recommends visiting a property before signing instead of relying on online information, and names Dabang, Zigbang and Naver Real Estate as popular search apps.

Health insurance, bank and phone

The portal states that international students staying more than six months are enrolled in the National Health Insurance. For degree students (D-2), enrolment starts from the date of foreigner registration, while general training (D-4) students enrol six months after entry. Enrolment is automatic: the insurance card and an enrolment guide are sent to your registered address in Korea. A premium reduction of 50 percent applies from March 2023 onward to eligible students who meet the income and property conditions, which the portal lists as monthly income of 300,000 won or less and a property tax base value of 135 million won or less.

For a bank account, the portal lists ID (passport, alien registration card or resident registration card), a seal or signature, and proof of the purpose of the financial transaction, with typical bank hours from 9:00 to 16:00. For a phone, the portal says an alien registration card is needed for postpaid plans, a passport for prepaid plans, and a student ID is also listed. Ask each provider about its own document rules.

A realistic monthly budget

The portal gives estimates for monthly living costs, not including tuition:

ItemMonthly estimate
Housing500,000 to 700,000 won (dormitory fees vary by occupancy and meals)
Food200,000 to 300,000 won
Transport50,000 to 100,000 won
Other, including phone, internet and health insurance100,000 to 200,000 won
Overall rangeabout 750,000 to 1,000,000 won

These are the portal's estimates, so your own total depends on city, housing and habits. The portal's savings tips are to use scholarships, share housing, cook instead of eating out and use public transport transfer benefits. It lists a university cafeteria meal at 5,000 to 15,000 won.

Part-time work needs permission

If you plan to work, do not start before you have approval. The portal explains that international students need permission for part-time work. The process includes an employment contract stating the hourly wage, a part-time employment confirmation form signed by the university's international student adviser, an application to immigration, and then approval or rejection. Eligibility and the permitted weekly hours depend on your programme, year and Korean level. The portal says working without permission or breaking the conditions can bring penalties.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Waiting until day 89 to register. Collect the enrolment certificate and proof of address early.
  • Forgetting the 15-day address report. Moving from a dormitory to a rental triggers it.
  • Signing a lease unseen. The portal advises viewing in person.
  • Underestimating deposits. Wolse and jeonse both involve a deposit, so ask the amount before you commit.
  • Starting a part-time job first. Permission must come before work.

Rules change: confirm with the official bodies

This is general information, not legal, immigration, medical or financial advice. Registration deadlines, fees, insurance rates and work rules are set by the Ministry of Justice, the National Health Insurance Service and your university, and they can change. Confirm current rules with HiKorea, the immigration office that covers your address, the National Health Insurance Service and your university's international office.
