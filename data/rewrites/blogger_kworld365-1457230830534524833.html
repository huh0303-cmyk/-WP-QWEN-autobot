Many K-pop hits are built by an international team rather than by one songwriter. According to The Korea Times, European composers often write the melodies, American producers refine the tracks, and Korean studios handle the final recording and vocals. Entertainment agencies organize "song camps" where composers from several countries produce dozens of demos in a few days, and Korean A&R teams then pick the strongest candidates and add Korean lyrics.

Step one: the song camp

The Korea Times describes song camps as intensive, multi-day sessions that agencies host so that international composers can generate demos quickly. The output is large on purpose: the article says these sessions produce dozens of demos, which gives the agency a wide pool to choose from.

The same report names Swedish teams as particularly prominent, known for powerful hooks and melody-centered pop songwriting, and says American and British songwriters and producers have become standard contributors at every level, from BTS and BLACKPINK to newly debuted groups.

Not every camp follows the high-pressure model. A separate Korea Times report covers a Seoul songcamp organized by the Korean artist Tasha, who said that when she heard K-pop companies talk about song camps, they sounded stressful, while to her the idea sounded romantic. Her version was a 24-hour event at a venue with six recording studios across three floors. According to the report, 21 Korean and international musicians took part, they made 16 new tracks, and 10 songs were selected for live performance. It is a small independent example, but it shows the basic format: many writers, a short deadline, many drafts, and a selection step.

Step two: selection and Korean lyrics

After the camp, Korean A&R teams review the demos. The Korea Times says they select top candidates and then add Korean lyrics while keeping pace with global market trends. This is the point where a track written abroad becomes a K-pop song: the choice of which demo fits which group, and the lyrics that fit the group's concept, are decided in Korea.

Step three: recording in Korea

The report summarizes the model with the phrase "composed in Sweden, recorded in Korea." Korean studios handle final recording and vocals, which is where the group's voices and performance direction are added to the imported composition. An industry representative quoted in the article said that for entertainment companies, preserving K-pop's identity while releasing music that can appeal to global listeners has become essential.

Why agencies work this way

The Korea Times gives two drivers for the global production model:

  • Global competitiveness. Success in overseas markets now determines a group's overall performance indicators.
  • Industrial efficiency. Multiple releases each year demand a steady supply of material, and song camps provide polished options quickly.

The article suggests the system is self-reinforcing: as long as international popularity continues, the collaborative model is likely to expand.

From studio to release: teasers and TV stages

A finished track is only part of a release. A K-pop production guide from KcontentHub describes the pre-debut phase as teaser content, such as videos, photos and behind-the-scenes material, released across YouTube, Instagram and other social media to build a fanbase before the official debut. The debut stage itself includes TV music show performances and a debut showcase, according to the same guide. That guide is a secondary, industry-explainer source, so treat its description as a general outline rather than a rule every agency follows.

Scale and concentration

Production at this level costs money, and that shapes who can do it. A Korea Times analysis, citing Circle Chart data, reports that seven of the top ten most-streamed songs in the previous year came from major agencies, compared with three songs from smaller agencies in the top ten in 2014. Capital for song camps, studios and promotion is one reason the biggest companies hold a strong position.

How to read credits and spot the pipeline yourself

  1. Open the credits on a streaming service or the album booklet and look at the composer and arranger names.
  2. Check whether several credited writers come from different countries, which is a sign of a camp-style process.
  3. Look for a separate lyricist credit for the Korean lyrics.
  4. Compare the release date with teaser dates to see how the promotion was staged.
  5. For the business side, check which agency owns the release and how its songs perform on charts such as the Circle Chart.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Assuming every K-pop song is made at a song camp. The sources describe it as a standard practice, not a universal one.
  • Treating a single story about one hit as the rule for the whole industry.
  • Repeating claims about who wrote what without checking the credits or the agency's official release information.
  • Mixing up the producer, composer and lyricist roles when reading credits.

Industry practices change

Production methods, credit conventions and chart figures change from release to release. The reports above were published as news and analysis, not as official industry standards. For the most current information on a specific song, check the credits listed by the artist's agency and the official release notes.
