If you are a foreign student or graduate in Korea and want to work here, your checklist has four parts: the right stay status for job hunting, an employer and contract that fit an employment visa category, the documents for a change of status, and the pre-employment checks before you sign. According to Study in Korea, the government portal for international students, a D-2 student can apply for a job-seeking (D-10) status, and a graduate can apply to change to an employment status such as E-7 once the requirements are met. The sections below set out each part.

Step 1: Check whether you can hold job-seeking status

Study in Korea says that D-10 is the status for people who want to engage in job-seeking activities at Korean companies, take part in short-term internships before official employment, join the OASIS start-up programme, or prepare start-up activities. Eligible applicants include international students who have obtained, or are expected to obtain, an associate degree from a Korean junior college, a bachelor's degree or higher from a Korean university, or who have completed or are expected to complete a research programme.

The page also lists an exclusion: someone who voluntarily resigned during an employment contract period within the past year without fault on the employer's side, and who had previously been granted D-10, is not eligible. Applications go to the local immigration office in charge of your residence as soon as your status changes, including an expected change.

For D-2 holders, the documents listed for a D-10 job-seeking change are the application form, passport, alien registration card and fee, proof of educational background or an academic transcript (not required if the immigration information system can verify it), a job-seeking activity plan, and a copy of a national technical qualification certificate where applicable.

Step 2: Match your job to an employment status

To change to an employment status, the portal says all three conditions must be met: you legally reside in Korea with D-10 or D-2 status, the field of employment corresponds to one of the employment categories such as Professor (E-1), Conversational Guidance (E-2), Research (E-3), Technical Guidance (E-4), Professional Employment (E-5), Artistic Performance (E-6) or Specific Activities (E-7), and you have signed an employment contract with the representative of the employer. D-2 holders must be prospective graduates who meet the requirements to change to D-10, unless they already hold a bachelor's degree or higher and meet the career requirements.

For E-7, the portal explains that holders of a bachelor's degree or higher from a Korean university can change without career requirements if the field of employment is related to their major, and the same applies to associate degree holders. It lists an occupation table for E-7: 15 manager occupations, 52 professional occupations, and further groups of semi-professional and skilled occupations. Before you apply for a job, check that the role fits one of these listed occupations.

Step 3: Check the limits on the employer and wage

Two limits matter when you pick an employer. First, the portal says additional E-7 hiring is restricted for domestic-focused businesses employing fewer than five nationals or where E-7 holders exceed 20 percent of the total national workforce, though advanced industrial sectors may hire up to 50 percent on the recommendation of the relevant authority. Second, changes of status are generally restricted if the employment contract offers less than 60 percent of the average wage of nationals with similar roles and experience, or if the monthly wage is below 1.5 million won.

These conditions are about the employer's situation, not your skills, so ask the employer early whether they have hired foreign staff on an employment visa before and whether they will provide the documents. The portal lists the documents for a change of status: the application form, passport, alien registration card and fee, the employment contract, employer documents such as a business registration certificate, a career certificate, a copy of any qualification certificate, and an employment recommendation letter or documents showing the necessity of the hire.

Step 4: Prepare your language and credentials evidence

The portal's employment page says most Korean companies require some Korean for daily communication and typically ask for TOPIK level 4 or above. It also publishes a Ministry of Justice standard that awards points for Korean proficiency: 20 points for TOPIK level 5 or higher, 10 points for level 3 or higher and 5 points for level 2 or higher. TOPIK scores are valid for two years as shown on the certificate, so check the date on yours before you submit it. The page also lists English tests, computer certificates and job-specific certificates as helpful, depending on the field.

Step 5: Before you sign

Study in Korea's pre-employment checklist includes opening a bank account, confirming enrolment in the four major insurances (National Pension, Health Insurance, Employment Insurance and Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance), and drafting a work contract that states salary, duties, benefits and leave. Under the rules the portal summarises, an employer must use a standard employment contract when hiring foreign workers, wages must be paid directly, in full and regularly at least once a month, and foreign workers cannot be treated unfairly because of their status.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Applying for roles outside the E-7 occupation table. Check the occupation before you invest time.
  • Forgetting the contract requirement. A status change needs a signed contract with the employer.
  • Ignoring wage floors. A contract below 60 percent of the comparable average or under 1.5 million won a month can block the change.
  • Letting a TOPIK certificate expire. The validity is two years.
  • Missing the timing. Apply for the status change as soon as it applies to you, and do not start work before it is approved.

Rules change: confirm with the official bodies

This is general information, not legal or immigration advice. Visa categories, occupation lists, wage floors and document requirements are set by the Ministry of Justice and can change. Confirm the current rules with HiKorea or your local immigration office, and consider asking a qualified immigration professional about your own case.
