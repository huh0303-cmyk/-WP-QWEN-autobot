International couples with a Korean partner usually face three separate things: relationship expectations that Korean dating culture sets early, a legal marriage that is a civil filing rather than a ceremony, and a wedding whose cost depends heavily on the venue and guest list. Handle them in that order, and write down what you agree on, because each step is easier when both partners have the same picture.

Understand the expectations Korean dating sets early

Korean dating tends to be more explicit than many foreign partners expect. A lifestyle article from KRSnap describes the phrase "우리 사귈까?" ("Should we date?") as a formal proposal: once it is accepted, exclusivity and public acknowledgment are expected right away, with less of a gradual middle stage. The same article notes that friend-arranged blind dates (소개팅) remain common because a mutual friend vouches for both people.

Couples also mark milestones. Tatler Asia reports that the 100-day anniversary (백일) is the first major one, followed by 200, 300 and 500 days, often with gifts or handwritten letters. Matching outfits (커플룩) and couple apps are mainstream, not unusual.

None of this is a rule. It is a set of habits your partner may assume you share. Ask rather than guess.

Build mutual trust by agreeing on the practical questions

Trust in a cross-border relationship mostly comes from shared information, not grand gestures. Talk through these before any paperwork starts:

  • Where you will live after the wedding, and for how long.
  • Money: who pays for what, and how the wedding will be funded.
  • Family involvement: which relatives attend, and which customs each family expects.
  • Language: what language you will use with in-laws, and who will interpret at the ceremony.
  • Paperwork roles: who gathers documents from which country.

On bills, the KRSnap article says men traditionally cover the first dinners while younger couples increasingly split costs ("Dutch pay"). Decide your own pattern instead of relying on a default.

Know that the legal marriage and the ceremony are separate

According to the U.S. Embassy in Korea, marriage in Korea is a civil procedure governed by Korean law, and a religious ceremony does not create a legal marriage. The couple registers at the local district office. For a U.S. citizen, the Embassy notarizes an Affidavit of Eligibility for Marriage, which must be translated into Korean when the other spouse is Korean. Two witnesses are listed on the filing. Citizens of other countries are told to contact their own embassy for requirements.

Many couples hold the ceremony first and register later, or the reverse. The order is your choice, but the legal date comes from the registration, so plan any visa or insurance steps around it.

Budget for the wedding itself

The Korea Consumer Agency surveys wedding prices. The Korea Herald reported a national average wedding cost of 21.6 million won in August 2025, based on a survey of 504 wedding service providers across 14 regions. The same report put Gangnam, Seocho and Songpa at 35.09 million won and the Gyeongsang provinces at 11.81 million won, and gave an average meal cost of 60,000 won per guest nationally and 88,000 won in the Gangnam area.

The Korea Times, citing the same agency, forecast a national average of 20.93 million won for May to December 2026 and said catering accounts for roughly 60 percent of total wedding expenses. It also noted couples are limiting invitations to close friends and family.

The practical lesson is that guest count and location drive the total. Invite fewer people, or choose a region outside the capital area, and the figure drops. If your guests are flying in, remember these averages cover Korean wedding services, not travel.

Prepare for traditional elements you may be asked to join

Remitly's overview of Korean weddings describes a modern wedding hall ceremony lasting about 20 to 30 minutes, with buffet-style dining. Guests give cash in white envelopes at the reception desk. After the main event, many couples change into traditional clothing for the pyebaek (폐백), when they bow to elders, receive advice, and, in a playful custom, catch dates and chestnuts thrown by the parents.

If you are not Korean, ask your partner's family which parts they expect and tell them which parts you would like to change. A short briefing for your own relatives also helps, since they may not know the gift-envelope custom.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Assuming a ceremony makes you legally married. Only the district office filing does.
  • Starting document collection late. Embassy affidavits and translations take time.
  • Comparing a quote to a national average without checking what the quote includes (meal, hall, photos, makeup, attire).
  • Skipping the money conversation until after deposits are paid.
  • Treating cultural habits as fixed rules in either direction.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This is general information, not legal or financial advice. Marriage filing requirements differ by nationality and district office, and cost figures change with each survey. Confirm the current list of documents with your embassy and the district office where you will register, and check current prices directly with venues.
