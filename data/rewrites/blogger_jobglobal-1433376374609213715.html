A Korean employer that hires a foreign national has to do three things well: confirm that the person's visa status allows the job before the offer, run a fair recruitment process that follows Korean hiring law, and keep the immigration paperwork current after the start date. This article walks through each stage so an HR team or a small business owner can see where the checks sit.

Confirm the visa status allows the job before you make an offer

The Korea Immigration Service's Visa Navigator guide sets out which activities each status covers. Holders of the professional employment statuses E-1 to E-7 may work within the occupational scope of their own status. The guide lists E-1 (professor), E-2 (foreign language instructor), E-3 (researcher), E-4 (technical instructor), E-5 (professional such as law, accounting or medical fields), E-6 (arts and entertainment) and E-7 (special occupations, described as professional, semi-professional or skilled workers).

The same guide says a D-10 job-seeker visa holder may do internship activities in fields that fit E-1 to E-7 status, and that part-time work is limited to graduates who meet specific criteria. A candidate on D-10 therefore cannot simply be put on payroll in any role.

The practical rule: ask for the candidate's passport and residence card early, note the visa category, and check that your job fits what that category allows. If it does not, the candidate needs a change of status first, and that is an immigration decision, not an HR one.

Why a wrong check is costly

The Seoul Immigration Office's "Easy and Concise Guide to Immigration Petitions" states that foreigners who work without authorization, including unauthorized part-time work, face a penalty of up to 30 million won or deportation. The guide also says employers who hire foreigners illegally are subject to penalties. It does not replace the Immigration Control Act, so an employer with a doubtful case should ask the immigration office or a qualified labor or immigration professional before the start date.

Keep the recruitment process itself fair

Korea's Fair Hiring Procedure Act applies to the way you advertise and screen, whatever the applicant's nationality. According to the English translation on the Korea Legislation Research Institute site, the Act says:

  • A job offerer may not place a false hiring advertisement, for example to collect ideas or promote a business site (Article 4).
  • Job ads or working conditions may not be changed to the applicant's disadvantage without justifiable grounds (Article 4).
  • Applicants may not be made to pay examination costs other than the expense of submitting hiring documents, unless an exception is approved (Article 9).
  • Applicants must be told without delay whether they are hired (Article 10), and may claim back their documents after that notice (Article 11).

The Korea Herald reported that the Act's Article 4-3 bars employers from asking for information unnecessary to the job, naming physical characteristics such as height and weight, place of birth, marital status, family members' jobs and property. The same article describes a Labor Ministry inspection of 627 establishments that found 281 violations, with 151 establishments committing infractions. Common problems included collecting family information and keeping disqualified applicants' documents.

The Equal Employment Opportunity and Work-Family Balance Assistance Act adds that an employer may not demand physical conditions such as appearance, height, weight or unmarried status that are not required for the duties (Article 7). The Framework Act on Employment Policy bars discrimination without a rational reason on grounds including gender, religion, age, social status, place of origin, alma mater, marriage, pregnancy and medical history (Article 19).

After the start date: reports and deadlines

The Seoul Immigration guide says an employer must report certain changes within 15 days. These include a foreign employee's resignation, death or disappearance, and changes to important items of the employment contract such as the contract period, employer, representative or workplace address. Reports can be made through HiKorea or by electronic fax, and the guide gives an administrative fine of up to two million won for failing to report.

On the employee side, the guide says registered foreigners must report a new address within 15 days. Foreigners staying longer than 90 days must register within 90 days of entry, according to the same guide and to the Korean Ministry of Foreign Affairs' ARC page. An employer cannot do this for the employee, but a reminder in the onboarding checklist helps.

Changing workplaces and roles

The Visa Navigator says an E-7 holder who wants to change or add a workplace within the scope of the visa must obtain permission in advance. E-9 holders can report workplace changes through e-application, and H-2 holders have their own declaration process. If an employee moves to a new department, a new site or a second employer, check with the immigration office before the move, not after.

A short employer checklist

  1. Write the job ad around real duties and qualifications, with no unrelated physical or family requirements.
  2. Record the candidate's visa category and confirm that it covers the job.
  3. Tell all applicants the result without delay and return documents on request.
  4. Set a calendar reminder for the 15-day reporting window for any contract change or departure.
  5. Ask about permission before any change of workplace for an E-7 employee.

Common mistakes to verify

  • Treating a student or job-seeker visa as a general work permit.
  • Asking about marital status, family or property in interviews or on application forms.
  • Changing contract terms after signing without a justifiable reason.
  • Waiting past 15 days to report a resignation or contract change.
  • Relying on an old guide: the Visa Navigator cited here is an older edition, so confirm current rules before acting.
