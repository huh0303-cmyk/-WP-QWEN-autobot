Foreign investors no longer need to register with the Financial Supervisory Service (FSS) before buying Korean-listed securities. According to the Financial Services Commission (FSC), individuals now open accounts with a passport number and corporations with a Legal Entity Identifier (LEI). That change is the main thing to build a Korea checklist around: the entry paperwork is lighter, but holding limits, reporting rules and disclosure language still shape how a portfolio is run.

What replaced foreign investor registration

The registration system dated from 1992 and was used to manage foreign investment limits. The FSC states that foreign investors had to register with the FSS before investing, and that the process was time-consuming and paperwork-heavy. The Korea Herald reported that the requirement was abolished with an effective date in mid-December 2023, following a proclamation in June of that year. Reports differ by a day on the exact effective date (December 14 in the Korea Herald, December 15 in the Korea Times), so treat it as mid-December 2023.

Under the new approach, a foreign individual uses a passport number and a foreign corporation uses an LEI when opening a securities account. The FSC adds that investors who already hold a foreign investor ID may keep using their registration number.

One practical warning: some older procedural summaries, including a Bank of Korea page on foreign portfolio investment procedures that was readable when this article was prepared, still list FSS registration as step one. If a broker or a guide tells you to obtain an investment registration certificate first, ask which rule they are relying on and for what date.

What the account-opening change did in practice

The Korea Times reported FSC figures covering December 15, 2023 to June 12, 2024. In that period foreign investors opened 1,432 new accounts, of which 1,216 were institutional and 216 were individual. The monthly average rose from about 105 accounts before the change to between 300 and 400 after it, roughly a threefold increase. These are account-opening counts, not a measure of how much money entered the market, so they should not be read as a performance signal.

What still applies after registration was abolished

Abolishing registration did not remove oversight. According to the FSC, foreign holding limits remain for roughly 35 items out of about 2,500 listed companies, and the authorities keep monitoring powers over designated securities. For a portfolio, that means a stock can be limited by the share of the company foreign investors are allowed to own, regardless of how easy the account was to open.

The same FSC release lists other measures announced alongside the abolition:

  • Omnibus accounts: a single account used by a global asset manager or securities firm to consolidate orders and settlement for several clients. The FSC described removing the requirement for end investors to report transactions at T+2 settlement, with an after-the-fact oversight system and a six-month pilot review.
  • OTC transactions: a wider range of over-the-counter transactions to be reported after the fact rather than examined beforehand.
  • English disclosure: a phased expansion. Stage 1 covered large KOSPI companies (assets of at least KRW 10 trillion, or at least 30% foreign ownership with assets of at least KRW 2 trillion), with material statements in English within three business days. Stage 2, listed as 2026 onward, extends to KOSPI companies with assets of at least KRW 2 trillion and expands the items disclosed.

The FSC release was a plan, with implementation dates given as targets. Whether each item is live for your account type is something to confirm with your broker or custodian rather than assume.

A pre-trade checklist for foreign investors

  1. Confirm your identifier. Passport number for an individual, LEI for an entity. If you hold an older foreign investor ID, ask the broker whether it stays linked to your account.
  2. Choose the account structure. Direct account, or an omnibus arrangement through a global manager or securities firm. Ask who is the account holder of record and who reports.
  3. Check holding limits for each target. Ask the broker whether a security is among the items with a foreign holding limit and how close the foreign share is to it.
  4. Check the disclosure language. Find out whether the company files material statements in English, and whether it falls in the first or second disclosure stage described by the FSC.
  5. Clarify OTC and settlement handling. If any part of the strategy uses over-the-counter trades or omnibus settlement, ask which reporting steps apply.
  6. Keep a record file. Save the account-opening confirmation, the identifier used, and any broker letter describing the rules applied to you.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Following a guide that predates the abolition. Check the date of any source that tells you to register with the FSS first.
  • Assuming no limits exist. The FSC states limits remain for around 35 items; check the specific security.
  • Treating a plan as a live rule. The omnibus, OTC and disclosure items were announced with timelines. Ask whether each is in force.
  • Mixing up account-opening ease with market access. Opening an account is one step; tax treatment, currency conversion and custody arrangements are separate questions this article does not cover.
  • Using the wrong identifier. The FSC describes passport numbers for individuals and LEIs for corporations; ask the broker which document it needs for your type of account.

Rules change: confirm with the official bodies

This article is general information for readers researching the Korean market. It is not financial, investment, tax or legal advice, and it does not recommend any security or strategy. Securities rules, holding limits and disclosure requirements are updated by the Financial Services Commission, the Financial Supervisory Service and the Korea Exchange, and brokers apply them in their own procedures. Before investing, confirm the current position with those bodies or with a licensed broker, and consult a qualified adviser for tax and legal questions.
