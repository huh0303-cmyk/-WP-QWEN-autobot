The Bank of Korea has raised its base rate twice in a row, to 2.75 percent in July and 3.00 percent on August 27, and the central bank has signalled that more increases are possible. For savers, that is generally good news in principle, but the Bank's own data as reported in the press shows that deposit rates have not moved one-for-one with the base rate, and loan rates have been climbing faster.

What the Bank of Korea decided

According to the Bank of Korea's decision statement of August 27, 2026, the Monetary Policy Board raised the base rate by 25 basis points to 3.00 percent from 2.75 percent. Six members voted for the increase and one, Hwang Kunil, preferred to hold at 2.75 percent. The Board said the domestic economy had continued to grow at a stronger-than-expected pace, supported by strong exports and a recovery in domestic demand, and that inflation was expected to stay above the target level for a considerable time. It called for "preemptive monetary policy action" to prevent inflation from spreading widely. It forecast consumer price inflation of 2.7 percent for this year and 2.3 percent for next year, with core inflation at 2.5 percent for both.

The previous decision, on July 16, raised the rate from 2.50 percent to 2.75 percent with the unanimous support of all seven members, and the Board said it would continue a policy stance consistent with further rate hikes. It also cited persisting financial stability risks.

The July statement named three drivers: stronger growth led by exports and investment, inflation expected to remain above the target, and persisting financial stability risks. It pointed to Korea's position in the AI supply chain, which supported semiconductor exports and investment, and to elevated energy costs and exchange rates that kept upward pressure on prices. That is why the Board treats the base rate as a tool against inflation and not as a tool aimed at savers or borrowers.

What has happened to deposit and loan rates

The Seoul Economic Daily, reporting Bank of Korea weighted-average interest rate data released on September 30, says the August deposit rate stayed flat at 3.21 percent, while the fixed mortgage rate rose 0.12 percentage points to 4.88 percent, its 11th consecutive monthly rise. The gap between lending and deposit rates widened to 1.19 percentage points in August from 1.06 in July.

So the pass-through is uneven. A higher base rate is a signal to banks, but each bank sets its own deposit and loan prices, and the flat August deposit figure shows savers cannot assume their rate will rise straight away. These figures are averages: the rate offered on a specific product, such as a promotional term deposit, can differ.

Savings: what to check

  • Your current product. Check whether your deposit earns a fixed rate until maturity or a rate that can change.
  • Term length. A longer fixed term locks in the current rate whether rates rise or fall afterwards. A shorter term lets you move to a better rate sooner. The Board's own statement is about further increases being possible, not certain, so avoid deciding on a forecast.
  • Deposit protection. The Korea Deposit Insurance Corporation says its limit is KRW 100 million per depositor, including principal and designated interest, per institution. Examples of products it does not cover include mutual funds, money market funds, bank-issued bonds and certificates of deposit. Check where your savings are held before chasing a higher rate.
  • Liquidity. Keep an emergency reserve in an accessible account before locking money away.

Loans: why rate rises hit borrowers faster

The Korea Times reports that household credit reached 2,019.8 trillion won in the second quarter, and estimates the latest 0.25-point rise will add about 1.8 trillion won a year to self-employed borrowers' interest costs, assuming 65.5 percent of their loans carry variable rates. The Seoul Economic Daily reported in an earlier article that fixed-rate mortgages were only 31.9 percent of new home loans in July, the lowest in over twelve years. Borrowers with variable-rate loans feel changes in market rates sooner than savers feel changes in deposit rates.

As an illustration only, a 0.25 percentage point rise adds 250,000 won a year in interest on a variable-rate balance of 100 million won, while the same rise on a 100 million won deposit that reprices fully adds 250,000 won of interest. The two do not usually net out, because the amounts and the timing of repricing differ.

A five-minute self-check

  1. List your deposits with the rate type, maturity date and institution.
  2. List your loans with the rate type and reset date.
  3. Estimate the yearly effect of a 0.25-point rise on each and add them up.
  4. Compare the rate you earn on cash with the rate you pay on debt.
  5. Decide whether to move money, repay debt or leave things as they are, and write the date you will review again.

Common mistakes to verify

  • Assuming your bank's deposit rate follows the base rate automatically.
  • Treating an average rate from a news report as the rate offered on your product.
  • Locking a long term for a small rate gain while you may need the money.
  • Comparing products by headline rate without reading the fees, conditions and maturity terms.
  • Forgetting the deposit protection limit when you hold large balances at one institution.
