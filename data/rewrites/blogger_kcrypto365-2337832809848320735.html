A Bank of Korea study released in early September concludes that heavy trading of dollar stablecoins can feed into a country's exchange rate once global trading platforms let people swap local currency for those coins directly. For Korea, the Bank says the link is currently weak, because the won cannot be used to buy stablecoins on major exchanges, but it warns that the picture could change if companies and foreign investors are allowed into the domestic crypto market.

What the Bank of Korea published

According to the Seoul Economic Daily, the report is a BOK Issue Note titled "Links Between Dollar Stablecoins and Foreign Exchange Markets", released on September 3, 2026 and written by Kim Ji-hyun of the bank's International Department. The Herald Business adds that Jo Sang-heum of the International Finance Research Team is a co-author and that the analysis covered 12 currencies.

The main finding

The Herald Business summarises the result this way: when a global intermediary such as Binance enters a market, stablecoin demand shocks feed through to exchange rates, strengthening the relationship between the supported currency and its dollar exchange rate. The Seoul Economic Daily gives two illustrations from the study:

  • After Binance enabled direct trading of stablecoins against local currencies, the stablecoin premium narrowed by 0.33 to 0.38 percentage points.
  • In Brazil, higher stablecoin demand translated into actual dollar purchases, and the real depreciated 0.12 percent against the dollar. The report observed similar patterns in Turkey and the euro area.

In plain terms, when it becomes easy to turn local money into dollar stablecoins, demand for those coins can become demand for dollars, which can weigh on the local currency.

Why Korea is described as insulated for now

The articles report that the won cannot be used to buy stablecoins directly on major exchanges and that Korea's market is made up mainly of retail investors trading assets they already hold, with limited participation by companies and foreign investors. As a result, the study says, a surge in domestic demand for dollar-backed tokens "is reflected only in stablecoin prices and does not directly affect the exchange rate."

That is a statement about the structure of the market today. It is not a prediction that nothing will happen, and the BOK does not say that stablecoins are unimportant for Korean users.

What could change the picture

The Seoul Economic Daily says the insulation could disappear if corporations and foreign investors gain wider access to Korean exchanges. Price gaps between domestic and overseas stablecoins would then narrow, which could create "a shock to the foreign exchange market". The Herald Business says the Bank cautioned that opening the market to companies and foreign investors could amplify won volatility.

The study's policy suggestion, as reported, is that digital asset regulation reform should proceed together with the internationalisation of the won, and that the foreign exchange market structure should be improved so it can absorb shocks. Kim Ji-hyun urged coordinating the two rather than treating them separately.

What this means for an individual holder

Reading the reported findings, a few practical points follow:

  1. The price you see for a dollar stablecoin on a Korean exchange can differ from overseas prices. The BOK describes those gaps as one channel through which exchange-rate effects could appear.
  2. Regulatory changes on who may trade, such as corporate or foreign participation, are the thing to watch, according to the study. Follow announcements from financial authorities rather than social media.
  3. A stablecoin is meant to hold a fixed value against a currency such as the dollar, but the BOK study is about market linkages, not about any one coin's safety. Check the issuer's reserve disclosures and the exchange's terms before holding any token.
  4. If you earn or spend in won but hold dollar-linked assets, you are exposed to the won-dollar exchange rate whatever the asset is.

How to read the study responsibly

The reports describe three limits worth keeping in mind. First, the evidence comes from other countries where direct local-currency stablecoin pairs already existed, so it describes what happened there. Second, the Bank of Korea's conclusion for Korea is conditional: it depends on whether the market opens to companies and foreign investors. Third, an Issue Note is a research publication from staff economists. It is useful for understanding risk, but it does not announce a change in rules. If you follow the topic, read the original note on the Bank of Korea's website and watch for announcements from the financial authorities.

Common mistakes to verify

  • Reading the Issue Note as official policy. It is a research note, and the Seoul Economic Daily article attributes its conclusions to the author.
  • Assuming "insulated" means "risk-free". The study says the current isolation depends on market structure that may change.
  • Treating one country's result as Korea's. The Brazil, Turkey and euro-area examples describe markets where direct local-currency pairs already existed.
  • Confusing a stablecoin's stable-price design with protection from exchange-rate or regulatory risk.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This article is general information about a published research note, not financial or investment advice. Virtual asset rules in Korea are being revised, and the details may have changed since the news reports I read. Check current rules with the Bank of Korea, the Financial Services Commission and the Financial Supervisory Service, and speak to a qualified financial adviser before making decisions about digital assets.
