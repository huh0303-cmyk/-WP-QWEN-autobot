The F-2-R is a regional residency status for foreigners who live and work in designated areas of Korea that are losing population. Based on the sources read for this article, a job or business in the designated area, a recommendation from the local government, and proof of Korean-language or integration-program level are the core elements. The sources disagree on some details, such as the language level and the number of eligible areas, so readers must confirm the current figures with the immigration authorities before acting.

What the F-2-R is meant to do

The job-matching site KoWork says the F-2-R "aims to encourage both foreigners and local residents to contribute to the sustainable development of local communities" in depopulated regions. Korea.net, the government's official site, reported that the Ministry of Justice expanded the list of eligible regions from 89 to 107 depopulated or declining areas. It described F-2-R as the status for region-specific foreign talent who can work anywhere within the depopulated regions.

That article also describes a related E-7-4R category for skilled workers, open to current E-9 and E-10 visa holders who have at least two years of residency and E-7-4 qualification. According to Korea.net, workers can move up to an F-2-R after three or more years in the target regions, accompanying family members can do simple labor, and the public contest selection process was replaced by business plan screening.

Where the sources differ

Check each of these directly with HiKorea or the Immigration Contact Center (1345):

  • Korean-language level. Korea.net says the F-2-R requires TOPIK level 4. The recruitment service Jobploy says the requirement was relaxed to TOPIK level 3 or higher, or completion of Social Integration Program level 3 or higher, or placement at level 4 or higher in the pre-evaluation, with an update dated August 3. KoWork lists the same alternatives. Jobploy adds that TOPIK level 3 alone does not get the visa issued, because all requirements must be met together.
  • Number of eligible areas. Korea.net reports 107 areas, while Jobploy still describes 89. Regional lists are set by the government and not every area recruits continuously.
  • Income test. KoWork describes personal income of at least 70 percent of the previous year's GNI per capita, while Jobploy refers to the living-wage standard of the metropolitan local government. These may be two ways of describing different routes, so ask which applies to you.

Eligibility elements most sources agree on

  • A qualifying education or income level: Jobploy and KoWork both describe a domestic associate degree or higher, or an income-based alternative.
  • A job in a designated industry in the designated area. KoWork says employment applicants must work in designated industries and meet the income threshold.
  • For business owners, KoWork lists a minimum investment of 200 million won, with the business operating in the designated area for the first two years.
  • Residence in the designated area in most cases, with limited exceptions that vary by region, according to KoWork.
  • A regional obligation. Jobploy states five years or more of residence and work or business activity in the area.

How to handle conflicting information

When two pages disagree, look for the date of each page, and note whether it comes from a ministry, a local government or a private recruiter. Government notices set the rule, and private sites usually summarise them. Ask the local government office of the area you are interested in what it currently requires, because regional recruitment announcements can differ from the national summary. Keep screenshots or printouts of the notice you rely on and the date you read it.

A step-by-step path for a job seeker

Jobploy outlines these steps:

  1. Check the regional recruitment announcements for the area you are interested in.
  2. Check your own eligibility: current visa, income or degree, and Korean-language level.
  3. Prepare an employment contract and arrange regional residence.
  4. Apply to the local government for a recommendation letter.
  5. Apply to immigration for the change of status or stay permit.

The contract matters at step 3. Ask the employer for a written agreement that states your duties, pay and working hours, and ask whether the company has hired through the regional visa route before. These are practical suggestions, not official requirements, but the employment contract is part of the application on all the sources reviewed.

What employers face

Korea.net says the maximum number of foreign staff was increased from 20 to 50 depending on business size, and that business plan screening replaced the earlier selection contest. Jobploy reports that a special exemption allows some small business owners with no Korean employees to hire one F-2-R worker if they have operated for three years or more, had annual sales of at least 100 million won in the previous year, and are in approved industries. Because these are secondary reports, employers should read the Ministry of Justice notice itself.

Common mistakes to verify

  • Applying on the basis of an out-of-date language requirement.
  • Assuming every listed area is hiring right now.
  • Signing an employment contract before the local government recommendation is confirmed as obtainable.
  • Treating a regional job offer as permission to move to a metropolitan area later. The status is tied to the designated region.
  • Relying on a recruiter's summary without reading the official notice.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This article provides general information, not legal advice or an immigration opinion. Regional visa rules are revised often and the details differ by region. Confirm the current rules with the Ministry of Justice and the Korea Immigration Service (HiKorea or the 1345 Immigration Contact Center), and consult a qualified immigration professional about your own case.
