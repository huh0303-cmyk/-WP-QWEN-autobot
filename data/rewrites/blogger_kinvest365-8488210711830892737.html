Before a first project inquiry in Korea, you should know what you are asking, who answers it, and which of your questions are commercial and which are administrative. KOTRA's Invest KOREA runs a free consulting service for foreign investors that covers procedures, registration, incentives and visas, so many administrative questions can go there first. This article lists what to prepare and what to ask.

Who to ask, and what is free

According to Invest KOREA, the Foreign Investment Consulting Center offers free guidance on foreign direct investment procedures, company registration, incentives, and administrative matters such as visa and driver's license processing. The Investment Consulting Center page says foreign investors and their family members can use the service by phone, fax, e-mail or face-to-face visit. Both pages list English, Chinese, Japanese and Korean support.

The center is at Invest Korea Plaza, 7 Heolleung-ro, Seocho-gu, Seoul, open Monday to Friday, 9:00 AM to 6:00 PM, closed 12:30 to 1:30 PM. The two Invest KOREA pages give different phone numbers (1600-7119 on the consulting center page and (82-2) 3497-1970 on the services page), so check the current number on the site before you call.

For problems after you have invested, Invest KOREA says the Office of the Foreign Investment Ombudsman has operated since 1999, with specialists in finance, law, taxation and labor.

Separate commercial questions from administrative ones

A first inquiry goes faster when you keep two lists. Commercial questions ask whether the project works as a business: customer demand, staffing, premises and suppliers. Administrative questions ask what the state requires: registration, tax treatment, permits and visas for key staff. A good answer to one does not settle the other. A product with strong demand still needs registration and, in some businesses, a permit.

Invest KOREA's incorporation procedure shows how many administrative steps exist. They include the investment notification, fund remittance, court registration, any needed permit, tax office registration, bank account opening and final registration of the foreign-invested company within 60 days of payment.

Prepare a one-page project brief

Send the same short brief to every adviser, so their answers can be compared:

  1. What the company will make or sell, and in which location.
  2. The planned legal form and the foreign investor's intended share of ownership.
  3. The planned investment amount and whether any of it is borrowed.
  4. The number of Korean and foreign staff you expect to hire.
  5. Whether your activity may need a permit, for example a health or food-related business.
  6. The date you hope to start, and any hard deadline.

The ownership share and the borrowing matter more than they look. Invest KOREA states that cash grants require a foreign investment ratio of 30% or more and exclude investments financed with long-term loans. Incentive eligibility can therefore turn on details you decide at the start.

Questions to ask in the first meeting

  • Does my investment need only a notification, or does it touch an area that needs approval?
  • Which incentives could apply to my size and sector, and what are the conditions?
  • Which documents will be needed, and which must be translated or notarized?
  • How long does each step take, and which steps can run in parallel?
  • What happens to my visa, and when can it be issued?

Invest KOREA notes that a residence visa is issued after company registration and puts the whole incorporation at about two weeks, so ask the adviser how that sequence applies to your case.

Plan the calendar

Invest KOREA's procedure gives approximate durations for several steps: two to three days each for fund remittance and court registration, and three to four days for tax office registration, within a total of about two weeks. Those are best-case figures for a straightforward case. A business that needs a permit, or an investment that needs screening, will take longer, so ask your adviser to give you a written timeline with the permit and any approval steps included.

It also helps to know the order of steps. The procedure says the investment notification comes first, before funds are sent, and that final registration of the foreign-invested company must happen within 60 days of payment. Missing that 60-day point is an avoidable error, so put it in your calendar on the day you remit the funds.

Reading cost and revenue estimates

When an adviser or partner gives you a cost or revenue figure, ask what period it covers, what assumptions sit behind it, and what is included or excluded. Ask for the date of the estimate in writing. A figure with no stated period or assumptions is only a number, not a plan.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Treating a verbal answer as final. Ask for the answer in writing and keep it with your records.
  • Skipping the permit check. Invest KOREA lists authorization as a step needed only for certain business types, so confirm whether yours is one.
  • Sending different facts to different advisers. Use one brief.
  • Relying on an old page. Confirm phone numbers, forms and thresholds on the current site.

Rules change: confirm with the official bodies

This is general information, not legal, tax or financial advice. Procedures, incentives and contact details change. Confirm current requirements with KOTRA's Invest KOREA, the tax office and the relevant licensing authority, and obtain case-specific advice from a qualified professional before you commit funds.
