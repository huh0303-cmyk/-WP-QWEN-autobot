An international student in Korea cannot start a new job just because an employer has offered one. Whether you can begin depends on your current stay status, what the job involves, and whether you need a permission or a change of status first. Before agreeing to a start date, find out which of three routes applies: permitted part-time work on a student visa, a switch to a job-seeking status, or a switch to a work status that matches the position.

Step 1: Identify your current status and what it allows

The Korea Immigration Service's Visa Navigator guide says employment is generally prohibited for students on D-2 and D-4 statuses, with limited exceptions. It describes part-time work as "limited to activities such as simple part-time labor at jobs usually performed by students, excluding specialized fields," with certain exceptions such as interpretation, translation, food service and tour guide assistance. It adds that students may work part-time after six months of residency in certain fields.

Study in Korea, the government's study portal, gives detail by program level: language-training students may work after six months, while associate, bachelor's and master's students can begin immediately, subject to permission. It states that Korean ability must be shown through TOPIK levels 2 to 4, Social Integration Program completion or a King Sejong Institute certificate, depending on the program level. Working-hour limits also differ by program level and language ability, so confirm your exact limit with your university's international office before agreeing to a schedule.

Step 2: Get permission before you work, even for part-time jobs

Permission is a separate step from the job offer. The Ajou University guide on D-10 status notes that holders "cannot work automatically" and must apply for separate permission from the immigration office before starting any work. Wonkwang University's international office describes the part-time process for students: submit an application with documents to the local immigration office, pay a fee of 20,000 KRW, and wait for review. It lists a certificate of enrollment and transcript from the school, plus a passport and foreigner registration certificate.

The Visa Navigator guide lists an example of a consequence. Among its violation examples, it says working part-time without a permit carries a maximum penalty of 30 million KRW. That edition of the guide is dated May 2023, so check the current penalty with the immigration office, but the message is clear: do not begin first and sort out paperwork later.

Step 3: Decide whether you need a different status

If the job is full-time or in a specialized field, a student status is unlikely to be the right fit. Study in Korea says graduates may transition to employment statuses from E-1 to E-7. It also says the job-seeking status D-10-1 allows up to two years to look for work after graduation, with internship employment permitted for a maximum of one year within that period.

It stresses that a change of status must happen before engaging in activities corresponding to the new status. In other words, the new status should be approved before the start date, not after it. The Visa Navigator guide includes flow charts showing paths such as student to professional status, and job seeker to professional status or startup. Which path fits depends on your degree, the employer and the occupation, and the guide itself says to consult HiKorea or the Immigration Contact Center on 1345 for case-specific requirements.

Step 4: If you are graduating, watch the timing

The Ajou guide says that after graduation, a D-2 visa remains valid for only up to 30 days, and recommends applying to change to D-10 two to three weeks before it expires. It lists a route through the HiKorea e-application, selecting a change of status of sojourn, or an in-person visit booked on HiKorea. Its document list includes the application form, passport and registration card, certificate of expected graduation, transcript, a job-seeking plan, proof of residence and living expenses, and a TOPIK certificate where applicable. It gives an application fee of KRW 130,000 and says D-10 is usually valid for six months and can be extended.

The specific documents and fees are from one university's guidance, so treat them as an example and check the current list on HiKorea. If your graduation date and your job start date do not line up, ask the immigration office how to avoid a gap in lawful status.

Step 5: Questions to settle with the employer and the immigration office

  • What are the exact duties? Ask for a written job description. A vague label can hide a role that your status does not allow.
  • What is the schedule and workplace? Hours and workplace may affect part-time permission.
  • Which status or permission applies? Part-time permission, a status change, or a new visa.
  • Who supplies which document? Employers often must provide documents for a work status.
  • When can work start? Get the approved date in writing, and do not describe a submitted application as an approval.

Write down the date, the office and the person who answered each question. If your situation changes, ask again rather than assuming an earlier answer still holds.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Starting work while the application is pending. Permission or approval comes first.
  • Using a friend's outcome as proof. Eligibility depends on your own status, school and job.
  • Missing the 30-day post-graduation window. Apply before your student visa lapses.
  • Ignoring reporting duties. The Visa Navigator guide lists obligations such as reporting a change of residence or a change of workplace within set deadlines, so ask what applies to you.
  • Relying on an old guide. Fees and rules in university pages and PDF guides can be out of date.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This is general information, not legal or immigration advice. Visa categories, fees, working-hour limits and documents change. Confirm your case with HiKorea, your local immigration office or the Immigration Contact Center (1345), and ask your university's international office for school-specific steps. For complex situations, consult a qualified immigration professional.
