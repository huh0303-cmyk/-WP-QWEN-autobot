For a Korean company that wants to hire skilled people from abroad or from the international student pool, the main routes are recruiting graduates already in Korea (often through a visa change) or recruiting from overseas with a visa issued for the job. In both cases the employer's job continues after the offer, because a new hire has deadlines for registration and address reporting, and the company has duties of its own.

Route one: international students already in Korea

Korea.net reports that around 200,000 foreign students study in the country, with projections reaching 300,000. To connect them with employers, particularly small and medium enterprises, the Ministry of SMEs and Startups and the Korea SMEs and Startups Agency launched the K-Work platform. According to Korea.net, it offers AI-powered job recommendations from resumes, one-stop help converting a D-2 student visa or D-10 job-seeker visa to an E-7-1 work visa, database links with universities to verify candidate credentials, and community networking by nationality. Korea.net described the platform as in beta at the time of writing.

The Korea Herald separately reported that the Study in Korea portal has become a permanent hub covering university programs, scholarships, visas and employment, with sections that include local-government support policies and region-specific visa schemes.

How the status change works for a graduate

A graduate's D-2 status lasts only a short time after graduation. Ajou University's international office explains that after graduation the D-2 visa is valid for up to 30 days, so a D-10 application should be filed before the D-2 expires. Its listed documents include a certificate of (expected) graduation, transcript, a free-format job-seeking plan, proof of residence and living expenses, and a KRW 130,000 fee. The same page notes that D-10 does not automatically allow work and that separate immigration permission is needed.

The Korea Immigration Service's Visa Navigator says a change from D-10 to a professional employment status (E-1 to E-7) requires meeting the employment criteria for the specific occupation. For the employer, that means checking early whether the offered role is one the candidate can legally take, rather than discovering the problem after the contract is signed.

Route two: hiring from overseas

The Visa Navigator describes a certificate for confirmation of visa issuance, which allows expedited visa processing abroad. It notes this is available for family members of D-7 to E-7 status holders in the context of applying at embassies overseas. Whether and how it applies to a specific candidate depends on the visa type, so the employer should ask the immigration office rather than assume.

The guide also states that E-7 holders must obtain permission in advance to change or add a workplace within the scope of the visa. Share this with new hires so they do not take a second job or transfer sites without checking.

Relocation support that is tied to legal deadlines

Several useful support steps are in fact rule-driven:

  • Alien registration. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs says foreigners staying more than 90 days must apply for a residence (alien registration) card within 90 days of arrival, at the local immigration office. A1, A2 and A3 holders are exempt. Offer paid time off or an escort for the appointment.
  • Address changes. The Seoul Immigration guide says registered foreigners must report a new address within 15 days to the immigration office or the local district office or community service center.
  • Health insurance and taxes. The same guide warns that unpaid taxes or health insurance premiums can restrict extension of the period of stay, so help the employee understand payroll deductions.
  • HiKorea. Online applications run through HiKorea, which the guide says is open from 7 a.m. to 10 p.m. on weekdays for applications.

The employer's own reporting duties

According to the Seoul Immigration guide, an employer must report a foreign employee's resignation, death or disappearance, and changes to important contract items such as the contract period, employer, representative or workplace address, within 15 days. The guide gives an administrative fine of up to two million won for non-compliance.

A first-90-days outline for a new international hire

  1. Before arrival: confirm with the immigration office which visa applies and whether a confirmation of visa issuance can speed up processing.
  2. First week: collect the passport and visa details for your records, and explain that E-7 holders need advance permission before changing or adding a workplace.
  3. Within 90 days of arrival: make sure the employee has an appointment for alien registration, since the deadline is 90 days from arrival.
  4. Whenever the employee moves: remind them that a new address must be reported within 15 days.
  5. Whenever the contract changes: file the employer's report within 15 days.

Common mistakes to verify

  • Assuming a degree from a Korean university automatically qualifies a graduate for the E-7 role offered.
  • Letting the D-2 expire before filing the D-10 application.
  • Starting work before immigration permission is in place.
  • Missing the 15-day reporting deadline when a contract changes.
  • Using an outdated guide: the Visa Navigator is an older edition, so check for current rules.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This article gives general information, not legal or immigration advice. Visa rules, fees and platform features change. Confirm current requirements with the Korea Immigration Service (HiKorea or the 1345 Immigration Contact Center) and, for labor questions, the Ministry of Employment and Labor. A qualified immigration or labor professional can review individual cases.
