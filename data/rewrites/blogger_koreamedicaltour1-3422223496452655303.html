A safe medical trip to Korea starts with three checks: whether you need a medical visa, whether your clinic or agency is legitimate, and what happens if something goes wrong. According to Medical Korea, the short-term C-3-3 visa allows stays up to 90 days and the long-term G-1-10 visa allows up to one year. The Korea JoongAng Daily reports that disputes and illegal broker cases have risen along with patient numbers, so choosing the provider carefully matters. This article is general information about planning, not medical advice.

Decide which visa you need

Medical Korea says the C-3-3 is a short-term visa for stays of up to 90 days, while the G-1-10 is for treatment lasting more than 90 days and is valid for one year with multiple entries. Both extend to immediate family members who assist with care. Whether your nationality needs a visa for your planned stay is a separate question, so check with the Korean embassy or consulate first.

The documents Medical Korea lists are a visa application form, a passport copy, medical diagnosis documents, a document from a Korean medical institution confirming your appointment for treatment or recuperation, a financial affidavit showing you can cover costs, proof of family relationship where relevant, and a photo for agency applications. Applications go to a Korean embassy or consulate. Medical institutions can also apply through the HUNET online system for a visa issuance certificate, after which the patient presents it at the consulate.

Fast-track options

The Korea Times reports that the Outstanding Facilitator for Foreign Patients program expanded to 112 certified providers from October 1: 89 medical facilities and 23 private agencies. Patients using certified channels get three-day electronic visa processing, financial document waivers and broader eligibility for accompanying caregivers. The paper notes this is only 1.6% of the 6,698 registered medical tourism providers, so most providers are not certified. The program is listed as valid through September 30 of next year. Ask any provider whether it is certified and how to confirm that.

Direct or through an agency

Medical Korea describes two routes for the visa. In the direct route, the patient applies at the consulate with the required documents. In the agency route, the medical institution submits a certification to the immigration office and the patient then presents it at the consulate. Either way, you need a document from a Korean medical institution confirming your appointment, so a visa cannot be planned before a provider is chosen. Ask the clinic which route it supports, how long it usually takes, and what it will provide in writing.

Choosing a provider

The JoongAng Daily reports that Medical Korea Support Center inquiries rose from 12,313 to 51,012 over four years, dissatisfaction and dispute cases rose from 22 to 214, and illegal broker cases rose from 13 to 67. The most common specialties in the dispute figures it cites were cosmetic surgery and dentistry. These are reported counts, not a rate per patient, but they show why checking matters.

  1. Ask for the hospital's or clinic's full legal name and address, and confirm it directly rather than through a broker.
  2. Ask who the treating doctor will be and what their specialty is.
  3. Ask for the treatment plan in writing, including what is excluded.
  4. Ask what follow-up care is available after you return home and who pays for it.
  5. Be cautious about any intermediary that cannot name the clinic until you pay.

Costs and paperwork

The Korea Times reports average spending by medical tourists of 6.73 million won per trip. Your own cost will differ widely with the treatment, so ask for a written quote that lists the procedure, anesthesia, hospital stay, medication, follow-up visits and any interpreter fee, and says what is not included. Medical Korea requires a financial affidavit for the visa, so keep your financial documents ready.

If something goes wrong

The Korea Medical Dispute Mediation and Arbitration Agency offers free consultation, mediation and arbitration. Its site says hospitals can add a clause in contracts with foreign patients that disputes follow the agency's arbitration, and lists a counseling line at +82-2-1670-2545 or +82-2-6210-0080 on weekdays from 9:00 to 18:00. The JoongAng Daily reports that foreign-patient cases at the agency rose from 113 to 133 over four years. Ask your provider whether such a clause is in the contract before you sign.

Keep copies of your diagnosis, appointment confirmation, quote and consent forms in a folder you can carry. If a clinic abroad later needs your records for follow-up care, having the Korean treatment summary translated and in hand makes continuing care easier. Ask the clinic what records it will give you at discharge.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Applying for the wrong visa length. Check whether your treatment may exceed 90 days.
  • Paying a broker before confirming the clinic. Verify the provider yourself.
  • Skipping the written plan. Verbal promises are hard to enforce.
  • Ignoring follow-up. Ask how complications would be handled after you leave.

Rules change: confirm with the official bodies

This is general information, not medical or legal advice. Visa rules, program lists and costs change. Confirm visa requirements with the Korean embassy or consulate that serves you and with Medical Korea, and discuss your treatment with a qualified doctor before you travel.
