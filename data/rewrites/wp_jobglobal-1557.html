The most effective way to hire international talent in Korea is to check, before you advertise, whether the role fits a visa category that exists for it, and then recruit where foreign candidates actually look. According to Study in Korea, the government portal for international students, graduates of Korean universities can change to an employment status such as E-7 if the job fits the rules, but the employer's size, headcount mix and pay level all affect whether the change is allowed. This article sets out those checks, the recruiting channels the portal names, and the contract rules to follow.

Check the role against the visa categories first

Employment visas are tied to occupations. Study in Korea lists the E-7 (Specific Activities) occupation table: 15 manager occupations, 52 professional occupations, plus smaller groups of semi-professional and skilled occupations, such as hotel receptionist, medical coordinator, chef and cook, and some manufacturing trades. The portal shows examples within the professional group, including software developer, web developer, translator and interpreter, designer, overseas sales representative and advertising and public relations expert.

Before you open a requisition, compare the job title and duties with that table. A role that does not correspond to a listed occupation is unlikely to support an E-7 change, whatever the candidate's skills. Candidates usually cannot fix this for you, so settle it on your side first.

Know the employer-side limits

The portal explains two limits that bear on how many foreign staff you can add. Additional E-7 hiring is restricted for domestic-focused businesses employing fewer than five nationals, or where E-7 holders exceed 20 percent of the total national workforce, though advanced industrial sectors may go up to 50 percent on the recommendation of the relevant government authority. Changes of status are also generally restricted if the contract offers less than 60 percent of the average wage of nationals with similar roles and experience, or a monthly wage below 1.5 million won.

These rules protect local employment and prevent low-wage misuse, as the portal puts it. Build your offer and your headcount plan around them, and confirm the current figures with the immigration office, since the portal says further details depend on the visa issuance certificate and the residency management guidelines for E-7.

Recruit from the graduate pool already in Korea

The portal says graduates and expected graduates of Korean universities holding D-2 or D-10 status can apply for a change to an employment status when they meet the requirements. For university graduates with a bachelor's degree or higher, there is no required connection between the field of employment and the major under the general rule, while the E-7 route for Korean graduates is described as open without career requirements when the job is related to the major. Because rules differ by category, ask your candidate which route they intend to use.

D-10 job-seeking status also allows short-term internships with training allowances before official employment, according to the portal. For an employer, an internship can be a way to assess fit before offering a contract, though you should check the conditions for the status with the immigration office.

Where to advertise

Study in Korea lists several recruitment channels for foreigners. They include KOTRA's Contact Korea, the Seoul Global Center, the Busan Job Information Network, Study in Korea's own employment platform, KoMate by Saramin with translations into 30 languages, Wanted Lab Global, the Ministry of SMEs and Startups' export-oriented training with employment linkage, Incruit and KOWORK. The portal's job search page offers an "E-7 Visa Support" filter, so marking a listing as visa-supported helps candidates find it.

It also describes job fairs for international students, which offer hiring information, counselling, matching programmes and mentoring from foreign professionals working in Korea, and university career support centres and international offices, which can offer internships. Some local governments are piloting a regional specialised visa programme, described as running in 28 regions, in which the region sets out the number of foreign workers it needs.

Write the posting and the contract carefully

State the duties, location, working hours, pay basis and visa support in the posting, since foreign candidates may be filtering on those fields. The portal's summary of the rules for foreign workers says employers must not treat foreign workers unfairly because of their status, must use a standard employment contract when hiring foreign workers under the Act on the Employment of Foreign Workers (with a fine of up to 5 million won for violations), and must pay wages directly, in full and regularly at least once a month. It adds that wages need not match those of domestic workers exactly, since they can vary with experience and productivity, but must be at least the minimum wage. The pre-employment checklist includes enrolment in the four major insurances and a written work contract covering salary, duties, benefits and leave.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Posting before checking the occupation. Match the role to the E-7 table first.
  • Ignoring the headcount limits. The 20 percent and fewer-than-five rules can stop a hire.
  • Offering below the wage floor. A contract under 60 percent of the comparable average or under 1.5 million won a month can block the change.
  • Leaving visa support unsaid. State it in the listing and tick the filter where available.
  • Treating the standard contract as optional. The portal's summary names a fine for not using it.

Rules change: confirm with the official bodies

This is general information, not legal or immigration advice. Visa categories, occupation tables, headcount ratios, wage floors and fines are set by the Ministry of Justice and the Ministry of Employment and Labor and can change. Confirm the current position with the immigration office that covers your workplace and the Ministry of Employment and Labor, and consider advice from a qualified immigration or labour professional for specific hires.
