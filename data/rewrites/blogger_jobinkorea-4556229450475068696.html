The most reliable way to prepare for a job interview in Korea is to build three short, true stories from your own experience: one where you solved a problem, one where you learned something new, and one where you worked with others. Each story should follow the same four-part order so you can adapt it to whatever question the panel asks.

Why stories work better than adjectives

Yale's Office of Career Strategy explains that behavioral interviews ask candidates to describe something they have done, with questions such as "Tell me about a time when...", because past actions are treated as a guide to future performance. KoWork, a job site for foreigners in Korea, lists questions that foreign candidates may meet in first interviews, including "During the project, what challenges did you face and how did you solve them?" and "If conflict arises among team members, how would you resolve it?" Both are questions that a prepared story answers directly.

The four-part structure

Yale recommends the STAR framework. In Yale's wording, you set the stage with the situation, describe your specific role in the task, explain the steps you took in the action, and state the result, quantifying it when possible. Yale advises keeping the account concise and focusing on your own contribution rather than the team's.

A skeleton to fill in:

  1. One sentence on the situation and why it mattered.
  2. One sentence on what you were responsible for.
  3. Two or three sentences on what you did, in order.
  4. One sentence on the result, with a number if you honestly have one.

Story 1: a problem you solved

Choose a real obstacle in a job, internship or project. Explain what went wrong or was missing, what you tried, and what changed. If the first attempt failed, say so and describe the second. Do not exaggerate the result; if you cannot measure it, describe it plainly, for example "the team stopped receiving duplicate orders".

Story 2: something you learned

Pick a skill or procedure that was new to you, such as a software tool, a legal process or a language. Describe what was difficult, how you practised, and where you used it afterwards. This answers the growth questions KoWork lists, such as which skills you have developed most and what you want to improve.

Story 3: a time you collaborated

Describe a shared goal and your specific part in it. If you are applying for a first job, Yale suggests drawing on internships, leadership roles, extracurricular activities and academics, so a university group project can serve as the example as long as you say clearly that it was a study project.

A worked outline you can adapt

The following is an invented example to show the shape of an answer, not a real case. Question: "Tell me about a problem you solved." Situation: a student club's event sign-ups were kept in three separate chat groups, and names were being missed. Task: I was responsible for the attendee list. Action: I moved sign-ups to one shared form, told members the deadline, and checked the list against the venue capacity the day before. Result: no one was left off the list and the final number matched the booking. Notice that it names one situation, one responsibility, a short sequence of actions and a plain result, in about a minute of speaking.

Practise for a Korean interview setting

KoWork's interview guide says candidates typically give a self-introduction of one to two minutes, and that smaller companies may ask personal or cultural questions. It suggests arriving 15 to 20 minutes early, wearing a conservative business suit, and avoiding criticism of past employers. It also recommends that you do not raise visa requirements or salary prematurely and, if you do not understand a question, ask politely for it to be repeated. These are one site's observations, not official rules, so use them as a starting point and check with the employer if you are unsure.

KoWork's list of Korea-specific questions includes why you want to work in Korea, how long you have lived there, and what the biggest cultural difference has been. Prepare short honest answers to these in the language of the interview. Ask the recruiter in advance which language will be used.

What an interviewer should not ask

You are not obliged to accept every question. The Korea Herald reports that Article 4-3 of the Fair Hiring Procedure Act forbids employers from requesting information unnecessary to the job, such as height, weight, place of birth, marital status, family members' jobs and property. The Equal Employment Opportunity and Work-Family Balance Assistance Act separately bars demanding physical conditions or unmarried status that the duties do not require (Article 7). If you are asked such questions, you can politely steer back to your qualifications, and you can contact the Ministry of Employment and Labor if you believe a rule has been broken.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Telling a team story with "we" throughout, so the panel cannot tell what you did.
  • Memorising a script word for word, which breaks down when the question is phrased differently.
  • Giving a result you cannot support. Stick to what actually happened.
  • Starting with a long company history instead of the situation.
  • Not researching the employer. Yale and KoWork both list company research among the basics.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This article offers general interview preparation, not legal or career advice. Hiring practices differ by company and industry, and laws are amended from time to time. For questions about hiring rules, check with the Ministry of Employment and Labor, and for visa questions use HiKorea or the 1345 Immigration Contact Center.
