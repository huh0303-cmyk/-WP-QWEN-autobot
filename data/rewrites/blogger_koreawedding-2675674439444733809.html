For a couple where one partner is Korean, three topics come up again and again: how dating is understood in Korea, how much Korean the foreign partner needs, and what traditional wedding customs may be expected. Dating habits are social conventions, the TOPIK exam is the standard yardstick for Korean ability, and wedding customs are a menu of options your two families can adjust.

How Korean dating is commonly described

Several lifestyle sources describe a few recurring patterns. KRSnap says friend-arranged blind dates (소개팅) remain popular because a mutual friend vouches for both people, and that the question "우리 사귈까?" ("Should we date?") works as a formal proposal after which exclusivity is expected immediately.

Tatler Asia reports that couples mark the 100-day anniversary (백일) and later milestones at 200, 300 and 500 days, often with gifts or handwritten letters, and that matching outfits (커플룩) are an ordinary way to show togetherness. It also lists romance-themed dates such as White Day on March 14, when men return gifts, and Pepero Day on November 11.

These are tendencies described by writers, not laws or universal behavior. Individual couples differ, so treat them as topics to ask about, not tests to pass.

What TOPIK measures

TOPIK (Test of Proficiency in Korean) is the standard Korean language exam. According to the overview on Wikipedia and the TOPIK Guide site, it has two tiers: TOPIK I covers levels 1 and 2 with a maximum of 200 points, and TOPIK II covers levels 3 to 6 with a maximum of 300 points.

  • TOPIK I: reading and listening. Wikipedia lists listening at 40 minutes with 30 questions and reading at 60 minutes with 40 questions.
  • TOPIK II: listening (60 minutes, 50 questions), writing (50 minutes, 4 questions) and reading (70 minutes, 50 questions), per the same source.

Both sources say results are valid for two years. TOPIK Guide notes the score is used for admission to Korean universities and for job-seeking at Korean companies and public institutions. Test dates, fees and registration differ between Korea and overseas, and the two sources do not fully agree on the months, so check the schedule on the official TOPIK website before planning.

How to use TOPIK when you are in a relationship with a Korean speaker

A language goal makes more sense when it is tied to a purpose. Some practical ways to set one:

  1. Decide whether you need a certificate (for a university, a job, or an application) or only conversation with family.
  2. If you need a certificate, pick the tier that matches your level: TOPIK I for beginner results, TOPIK II for intermediate to advanced.
  3. Note the two-year validity before you book, so the score is still valid when you submit it.
  4. Practice with your partner in short sessions, but use official past papers for exam format.

If you are applying for any visa, do not assume a particular TOPIK level is required. Language requirements depend on the visa category and your circumstances, so ask the immigration office or consulate.

Traditional wedding customs you may encounter

Korean weddings today often combine a short hall ceremony with traditional elements. Remitly's overview says hall ceremonies typically last 20 to 30 minutes with buffet-style dining, and guests give cash in white envelopes at a reception desk. It also describes the ham, a gift box delivered to the bride's family the night before, and the hanbok, in which red is associated with good fortune and blue with loyalty.

The pyebaek (폐백) is the best-known custom for newcomers. Wikipedia describes it as a ceremony in which the newlyweds pay formal respects to elders with a deep bow, offer symbolic foods such as jujubes and chestnuts, and receive marriage advice. Parents toss the fruit for the bride to catch in her skirt, a gesture tied to hopes for children. It was originally for the groom's family only, and modern ceremonies often include the bride's family too.

Agreeing on which customs to keep

Because nothing in these customs is legally required, the decision belongs to the couple and the two families. A simple way to approach it:

  • List each custom your partner's family expects and ask what it means to them.
  • Mark which ones you are happy to join, which you would change, and which you would skip.
  • Explain the customs to your own relatives ahead of time, especially the gift envelopes.
  • Ask the venue whether a pyebaek room, hanbok rental and an interpreter are available.

Common mistakes and what to verify

  • Treating blog descriptions of dating culture as fixed rules.
  • Booking a TOPIK date without checking the official schedule and the validity window.
  • Assuming a TOPIK score alone satisfies a visa or admission rule.
  • Skipping the conversation with both families about customs.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This is general cultural and educational information, not legal advice. TOPIK schedules and fees, and any language requirement tied to a visa, are set by the organizers and immigration authorities and can change. Confirm current details on the official TOPIK website and with the Korea Immigration Service or your nearest consulate.
