A student who joins a Korean university through an exchange agreement between their home school and the Korean host needs an exchange student visa (D-2-6) before arriving, and the visa depends on documents from both universities. This article follows that route from admission to arrival, using a Korean university's exchange guide and a Korean embassy's document checklist as examples.

What an exchange program means in practice

In an exchange program the student remains enrolled at the home university and studies for a set period at a partner institution under an agreement between the two. This is visible in the paperwork: the document checklist published by the Korean visa application centre in Mongolia asks for a copy of the exchange agreement between the Mongolian and the Korean institution, plus proof of current enrolment and academic records from the home institution. Credit recognition, tuition arrangements and the length of stay are set by the universities, so read your own school's agreement and ask the international office how credits will be counted.

Step 1: get the admission certificate from the host university

Myongji University's exchange guide says the visa application requires a Certificate of Admission, which the university's Office of International Affairs prepares. It advises checking that the certificate lists the correct name, birth date, gender, passport number and exchange duration, and says errors must be corrected by the university before the visa application is resubmitted. The same guide says the admission certificate is valid for only three months from issuance.

Step 2: apply for the D-2-6 visa before travelling

Myongji's guide states that exchange students must have a valid student entry visa before arriving in Korea. It says student visas are single-entry and remain valid for 90 days from issuance, that processing takes time, and that you should not book flights until the visa is approved. It also warns against entering Korea first as a tourist or under visa-free tourist conditions, because a student visa will not be granted from within Korea in that case. Other universities may have different rules, so follow your host university's instructions and the Korean embassy or consulate for your country.

The Mongolia checklist shows the kind of documents a mission may ask for:

  • A visa application form, passport, a recent photo and the visa fee. The checklist lists a fee of 60 USD, which applies to that mission only.
  • The host university's acceptance letter and the exchange agreement.
  • Proof of enrolment and transcripts from the home institution.
  • A sponsor's letter covering expenses, with identification and income proof.
  • A tuberculosis test result from an authorised hospital and a criminal record certificate issued within 45 days.

The checklist also lists language competence, TOPIK level 3 for technical or vocational programs and TOEFL 510 for English. Those figures are specific to the document I read and may not apply to your program, so ask the Korean mission for your country for its own list.

Step 3: register after you arrive

The Ministry of Foreign Affairs says foreigners who will stay in Korea for more than 90 days must apply for an alien registration card within 90 days of arrival, at the local immigration office. The Seoul Immigration Office's guide adds that registered foreigners must report a new address within 15 days. Your university's international office normally helps exchange students through this step, but you remain responsible for the deadlines.

Where to find programs and information

The Korea Herald reports that the Study in Korea portal has become a permanent hub with information on university programs, scholarships, visas and employment, organised into sections for Korean language programs, English-taught degrees, vocational colleges and local-government initiatives. For an exchange, though, your first stop is the international office at your home university, because only partner institutions of your school are available to you.

Questions for the international office

Before you apply, put these questions to your home university's international office and, once admitted, to the host:

  • Which Korean partner universities does our agreement cover, and for how many semesters?
  • Which documents will you issue, and how long are they valid?
  • Does the host help with the alien registration appointment after arrival?
  • Which courses are taught in a language I can use?

Preparing beyond the paperwork

  1. Ask your home office which Korean partners are available and for how long.
  2. Confirm in writing how your courses will transfer back.
  3. Check the language of instruction for the courses you want.
  4. Budget for the costs the sponsor letter must cover, and ask whether the university offers housing.
  5. Collect the health and background documents early, since certificates can take time and may expire.

Common mistakes to verify

  • Entering Korea on a tourist basis and planning to switch to a student visa afterwards.
  • Booking a flight before the visa is approved.
  • Letting the admission certificate or a criminal record certificate expire before applying.
  • Assuming one country's checklist applies to yours.
  • Skipping the credit-transfer conversation until after you return.

Rules change: confirm with the official body

This article is general information and not immigration or legal advice. Visa documents, fees and language requirements differ by country, mission and university, and they change. Confirm the current rules with the Korean embassy or consulate that serves you, HiKorea or the 1345 Immigration Contact Center, and the international office of the universities involved.
